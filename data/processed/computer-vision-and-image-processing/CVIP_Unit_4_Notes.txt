1 Feature Descriptors and Matching
Feature descriptors and matching form the foundation of object recognition and scene analy-
sis. They enable the identification of distinctive patterns in images that remain stable under
transformations such as scaling, rotation, illumination changes, and viewpoint variations.
1.1 Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT)
The Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT), proposed by David Lowe (1999, 2004), ex-
tracts distinctive keypoints and descriptors from images that are robust to scale, rotation,
and moderate affine transformations.
1.1.1 Key Steps in SIFT
1. Scale-space extrema detection: Keypoints are detected as maxima/minima of the
Difference-of-Gaussians (DoG) function across scales:
D(x,y,σ) = (G(x,y,kσ)−G(x,y,σ))∗I(x,y)
where G is the Gaussian kernel, I is the image, and k is a constant factor.
2. Keypoint localization: Taylor series expansion is used to refine location, scale, and
remove unstable points with low contrast or along edges.
3. Orientation assignment: Each keypoint is assigned a dominant orientation based
on local image gradient:

4. Descriptor generation: For each keypoint, a 16×16 neighborhood is divided into

1.1.2 Properties of SIFT
• Invariant to scale, rotation, and moderate affine changes.
• Robust against illumination changes and occlusion.
• Computationally expensive for large-scale applications.
1.2 Speeded-Up Robust Features (SURF)
SURF (Bay et al., 2006) was developed as a faster alternative to SIFT while maintaining
robustness.

3.4 Random Forests
Random Forests are ensembles of decision trees, combining multiple weak learners to form
a strong classifier.
3.4.1 Key Concepts
• Bootstrap Aggregation (Bagging): Train each tree on a random bootstrap sample
of the dataset.
• Random Feature Selection: At each split, choose the best split among a random
subset of features.
• Prediction: majority vote for classification or average for regression.
3.4.2 Advantages
• Robust to noise and overfitting compared to single trees.
• Works well with high-dimensional data and nonlinear boundaries.
• Used in vision for image classification, feature selection, and retrieval.
3.5 Applications in Vision
• SVMs: face detection (e.g., HOG + SVM in Dalal–Triggs pedestrian detector).
• Decision Trees: interpretable models for small-scale recognition tasks.
• Random Forests: landmark detection, medical image classification, keypoint recogni-
tion.
4 Deep Learning Architectures

widely used in computer vision for feature learning, sequence modeling, and generative
modeling. This section introduces autoencoders, recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and
generative adversarial networks (GANs).

4.3 Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
GANs (Goodfellow et al., 2014) are generative models consisting of two competing networks.
4.3.1 Architecture
• Generator G(z): maps random noise vector z ∼ p(z) to synthetic image.
• Discriminator D(x): distinguishes real images from generated ones.

4.3.3 Training Dynamics
• Adversarial training: D improves at classification while G learns to fool D.
• Balance between G and D is crucial; instability can lead to mode collapse.
4.3.4 Variants
• Conditional GAN (cGAN): generation conditioned on labels y.
• DCGAN: deep convolutional GAN using CNN layers in G and D.
• CycleGAN: learns mappings between two unpaired domains (e.g., summer ↔ winter
scenes).
4.3.5 Applications in Vision
• Image synthesis and data augmentation.
• Super-resolution and image-to-image translation.
• Domain adaptation for cross-modality medical imaging.
