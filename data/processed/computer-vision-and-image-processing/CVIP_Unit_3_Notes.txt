1 Introduction to Stereo Vision
Stereo vision involves using two images of the same scene taken from different viewpoints to
extract 3D information. The core idea is to compute the depth of scene points by analyzing
their projections onto two image planes.
2 Epipolar Geometry
Epipolar geometry describes the intrinsic projective geometry between two views of a scene.
2.1 Terminology
• Epipole: The point of intersection of the baseline with the image plane.
• Epipolar Line: For a point in one image, the corresponding point in the other image
lies along a line known as the epipolar line.
• Baseline: The line joining the centers of projection (camera centers).

6 Introduction to Structure from Motion (SfM)
Structure from Motion (SfM) is a computer vision technique that reconstructs the 3D struc-
ture of a scene and estimates camera motion using a sequence of 2D images. Unlike stereo
vision, SfM does not require multiple calibrated cameras; it works with a single moving
camera.
7 Feature Tracking Across Frames
Tracking visual features is a prerequisite to estimate motion and build a 3D scene model.
7.1 Feature Detection
Common corner/feature detectors:
• Harris Corner Detector
• Shi–Tomasi Detector
• SIFT (Scale Invariant Feature Transform)
• ORB (Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF)
7.2 Feature Matching and Tracking
• Optical Flow: Tracks pixels between consecutive frames assuming brightness con-
stancy.
• KLT Tracker (Kanade-Lucas-Tomasi): Tracks feature points using local image
patches.

W = MS

• M: motion matrix (2f ×3)

9 Applications in Scene Understanding
SfM supports numerous vision-based systems:
• 3D Reconstruction: Mapping buildings, terrain, archaeological sites
• Autonomous Navigation: SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping)
• AR/VR: Recovering camera pose and 3D geometry for realistic overlays
• Cinematic and Gaming Visualization: Digitally recreate physical spaces
SfM is a powerful monocular approach for recovering 3D structure from image sequences.
It builds on robust feature tracking, motion estimation, and geometric triangulation to infer
scene depth and motion trajectory.
10 Introduction to Optical Flow
Optical flow is the pattern of apparent motion of image objects between two consecutive
frames caused by the movement of object or camera. It is widely used in video compression,

13 Motion Segmentation
Motion segmentation partitions image sequences into regions corresponding to distinct mo-
tion patterns.
13.1 Approaches
• Background subtraction: Static background model with foreground detection.
• Optical flow clustering: Grouping pixels with similar motion vectors.
• Layered motion models: Multiple affine or homographic motion models.
14 Applications of Optical Flow
• Human activity recognition
• Object tracking in video
• Autonomous vehicle perception
• Surveillance and behavior analysis
Optical flow provides dense motion field estimation, while methods like Lucas–Kanade
and Horn–Schunck offer sparse and dense solutions respectively. These techniques form the
foundation for motion-based segmentation and 3D analysis.

16 Calibration Techniques
16.1 Zhang’s Method
One of the most widely used camera calibration techniques, based on multiple views of a
planar checkerboard pattern.
Steps:
1. Capture several images of a known planar calibration grid.
2. Detect 2D image points corresponding to known 3D positions on the grid.
3. Estimate homographies H from plane to image.
4. Solve for intrinsic matrix K, then recover R and t.
