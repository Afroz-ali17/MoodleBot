Introduction to Quantum Technologies and Applications
In physics, “quantum” refers to the smallest discrete
unit, especially energy or other physical properties.
The concept comes from quantum theory, which
describes how particles and energy behave at the
smallest scales (atomic and subatomic levels).
More specifically:
•The word quantum comes from Latin, meaning “how
much” or “amount.”
•In quantum physics (or quantum mechanics), it
describes how certain physical properties—like energy,
momentum, or angular momentum—can only take on
specific, discrete values rather than any value
continuously.
•For example, the energy of electrons in an atom is
quantized, meaning electrons can only exist at certain
energy levels, not in between.
•This idea contrasts with classical physics, where
properties like energy are treated as continuous.

The Quantum Revolution: A Paradigm
Shift in Physics
The early 20th century marked a profound shift in our understanding of the universe,
transitioning from the deterministic, continuous world of classical physics to the
probabilistic, discrete realm of quantum mechanics. This presentation explores the
fundamental principles that underpin this revolution and its far-reaching implications
for science, technology, and society.

electromagnetic waves. However, it faltered when attempting to explain observations at the atomic and subatomic levels, leading to a series of experimental
puzzles that classical theories could not resolve.

• For instance, an electron can be in a superposition of spinning both clockwise

understanding quantum phenomena like interference and entanglement.

Entanglement
Entanglement occurs when a group of particles (e.g., electrons, photons) interact or are generated in a
way that their quantum states become interdependent, even when they are separated by vast distances.
Thisphenomenon isone of the key distinctionsbetween quantummechanics and classical physics.
Example: Imagine you have two coins. In classical physics, if you flip them, each coin has a definite
state (heads or tails). With entangled particles, it's different. Before measurement, each particle exists in
a superposition of possible states (e.g., both "spin up" and "spin down" simultaneously). When you
measure the state of one entangled particle, its superposition collapses to a definite state, and
instantaneously, the entangled partner's state also collapses into a correlated definite state, no matter
how far apart theyare.
How it works
 Entanglement arises when particles are generated, interact, or share proximity in a way that creates
an inseparable, unified quantum state.
 Imagine two entangled photons with random polarizations. If you measure one photon and find it to
be vertically polarized, you instantly know the other photon will also be vertically polarized,
regardless of how far apart they are.
 This instantaneous correlation suggests a connection beyond classical understanding.
 Experiments have repeatedly shown that these correlations cannot be explained by "local hidden
variable theories," which suggest pre-determined properties within particles.
Key characteristics
•Non-separability: The entangled system must be described by a single quantum state (a "joint state"), not as individual particle states.
•Strong correlations: Measurements on entangled particles are perfectly correlated or anti-correlateddepending on the entanglement type,
exceeding classical correlation limits.
•Decoherence: This phenomenon is fragile, and any interaction with the environment can cause the entangled particles to lose their link, collapsing
the unified state.
•Non-locality (debated): While not definitively proven as faster-than-light communication, the instantaneous nature of entanglement appears to
challenge the principle of locality, which states that influences can only travel at or below the speed of light.

Significance and applications
• Quantum Information Science: Entanglement is a critical resource for quantum computing, cryptography, and communication.
• Quantum Computing: Entanglement allows quantum computers to perform computations that are impossible for classical
computers, as qubits (quantum bits) can exist in multiple states simultaneously.
• Quantum Cryptography: The properties of entanglement ensure that any spying attempt disturbs the entangled state, revealing the
presence of a third party, thus enhancing security.
• Quantum Teleportation: Entanglement can be used to transfer the quantum state of one particle to another distant particle.
• Quantum Metrology and Sensing: Entanglement can boost the sensitivity of measurements in various fields, like spectroscopy and
medical imaging.
Uncertainty Principle
Formulated by Werner Heisenberg, the Uncertainty Principle states that there are fundamental limits to the
precision with which certain pairs of complementary properties of a particle, such as position (x) and
momentum (p), can be known simultaneously. The more precisely you measure one, the less preciselyyou can
know the other.
Mathematically, for position and momentum: ΔxΔp≥2ℏ , where Δx is the uncertainty in position, Δp is the
uncertaintyin momentum, andℏ (h-bar) isthe reduced Planck constant (h/2π).
Implication: It's not a limitation of our measuring instruments but an intrinsic property of nature at the
quantum level. Particles do not have a definite position and momentum simultaneously; their "existence" is
inherentlyprobabilistic.Thismeans thatquantumevents are not entirelydeterministic in the classical sense.
Wave-Particle Duality
Wave-particle duality is the concept that all matter and energy exhibit properties of both waves and particles.
Light, which was classically understood as a wave, shows particle-like behavior (photons) in phenomena like
the photoelectric effect. Conversely, particles like electrons, classically understood as discrete entities, exhibit
wave-like behavior,as demonstrated by electron diffraction experiments.

Quantum States and Measurement: The Nature of Observation
In quantum mechanics, the state of a system is described by a mathematical entity called a
wave function (ψ), which contains all the probabilistic information about a particle. The
Born rule states that the square of the magnitude of the wave function (|ψ|2 ) gives the
probability density of finding the particle in a particular state or location.
The Measurement Problem
• Superposition before Measurement: Before observation, a quantum system exists in
a superposition of multiple possible states. For example, an electron's spin might be in
a superposition of "spin-up" and "spin-down."
• Wave Function Collapse: Upon measurement, the wave function instantaneously
"collapses" from a superposition into a single, definite state. The system is then found
in only one of its previously superposed states, chosen randomly according to the
probabilities encoded in the wave function.
• Probabilistic Outcomes: Even with identical quantum systems and measurements,
individual outcomes can differ. The wave function only predicts the probability of
each possible outcome, highlighting the characteristic randomness fundamental to
quantum mechanics.
• Observer Problem: The role of the "observer" or "measuring device" in causing this
collapseis a central and debated aspect of quantum mechanics. What exactly
constitutes a "measurement" and when the collapse occurs are open questions.
• Non-deterministic: Unlike classical mechanics, where future states are precisely
predictable, quantum mechanics is fundamentally probabilistic. We can only predict
the probability of different outcomes.
In quantum mechanics, the act of observing a system is not a passive process; instead, it actively influences the reality being observed, forcing the system
into a definite state. This challenges the classical idea that an objective reality exists independently of any observer and their interaction with it.

The Concept of Quantization: Discrete Energy Levels
Quantizationis a fundamental principle in quantum mechanics stating that certain physical
quantities can only take on discrete, specific values, rather than a continuous range. This is a
Stark contrast to classical physics and a foundation stone of the quantum world.
Key Aspects of Quantization
• Discrete Energy Levels:The most prominent example is the energy levels of electrons in
atoms. Electrons are not free to orbit at any radius or have any arbitrary energy; they are
restricted to specific, allowed energy levels. Transitions between these levels involve the
emission or absorption of photons with energies precisely equal to the energy difference.
• Planck's Constant (h): Max Planck first introduced this concept to explain black-body
radiation, proposing that energy is exchanged in discrete packets proportional to frequency,
with h ( 6.626 10-34{ Js}) as the constant of proportionality.
• Angular Momentum and Spin: In quantum mechanics, the angular momentum of
particles(like electrons in orbitals or with intrinsic spin) is also quantized, taking on
integer or half-integer multiples of \hbar(reduced Planck's constant).
Consequences of Quantization
• Atomic Stability:Quantization prevents electrons from continuously losing
energy and spiraling into the nucleus, ensuring that atoms are stable.
• Spectroscopy:It explains why atoms and molecules absorb and emit light only at
specific wavelengths, leading to unique spectral "fingerprints" used for
identification in fields ranging from chemistry to astrophysics.
• Band Theory of Solids:The quantization of electron energies in solids gives rise
to energy bands, which fundamentally explains the electrical conductivity of
materials (conductors, semiconductors, insulators).
Quantization is not merely a theoretical construct; it is a fundamental property of nature at the microscopic scale that underpins the
stability and behavior of all matter, shaping the very fabric of our universe.
