Quantum computers have long been considered the future of computing. Quantum computing harnesses the power
of quantum mechanics, offering exponential processing capabilities that far exceed traditional computers.
1. Quantum Computer Applications in Healthcare and Drug Discovery
Traditional drug discovery processes involve trial and error in the lab, but quantum computers can simulate
molecular interactions at a level of detail that classical computers cannot achieve.
This allows researchers to design new drugs, predict molecular behaviors, and optimize treatments much faster and
more accurately. Quantum simulations can speed up the development of personalized medicine, creating tailored
treatment plans for individual patients based on their genetic makeup.
Real-World Example:
In collaboration with pharmaceutical company Boehringer Ingelheim, Google has demonstrated that quantum
computers have the potential to simulate key components of Cytochrome P450—a vital enzyme in the human
body—with greater efficiency and precision than classical methods. This enzyme plays a crucial role in drug
metabolism. Accurately modeling its structure could significantly enhance how we predict drug interactions and
treatment efficacy, accelerating progress in pharmaceutical research.
Also, Companies like Google and IBM have begun using quantum computing to
simulate complex protein folding and interactions, a crucial aspect of drug
development. These advancements hold the promise of accelerating the
discovery of new therapies for diseases like cancer, Alzheimer’s, and viral infections.
2. Quantum Computer Applications in Materials Science and Energy
Quantum computers are expected to transform material science by enabling precise simulations of molecular and
atomic interactions—something classical computers struggle with, especially in complex materials. This
advancement could lead to the discovery of new materials with enhanced properties for use in electronics,
manufacturing, aerospace, and energy storage.
Better Batteries
As global demand for energy and efficient energy storage continues to rise, Google is exploring how quantum
computers could accelerate the discovery of advanced materials.
Case in point: In collaboration with chemical company Badische Anilin- und Soda-Fabrik (BASF), Google has
studied how quantum algorithms may enable more precise simulations of Lithium Nickel Oxide (LNO)—a promising
battery material with a lower environmental impact than the widely used lithium cobalt oxide.
Despite its potential, LNO remains difficult to synthesize on an industrial scale, partly due to the complexity of its
chemical behavior. By simulating its quantum properties, Google aims to support more sustainable battery
innovation, including the development of cobalt-free alternatives and improvements in manufacturing efficiency.

Moderna and IBM Quantum are working toward a quantum-enabled biotechnology pipeline.
Moderna is a leading pharmaceutical and biotechnology company. A pioneer in messenger RNA (mRNA) medicines
and vaccines, it uses mRNA molecules that play a crucial role in the body to treat and prevent diseases. Today,
Moderna is exploring the application of quantum computing in the design of mRNA medicines through a research
and technology partnership with IBM.
The human body contains more than 1,00,000 types of proteins, and each protein is derived from mRNA. For
decades, scientists have known that mRNA has the potential to form the basis of a new class of medicines that could
address illnesses at the most fundamental level of cellular function. Moderna has been a leader in putting that
insight into action.
Moderna has a fast, scalable approach to this molecular chemistry work, but the company is always looking for ways
to improve the process of developing mRNA medicines. This drive has led Moderna to develop its quantum
computing expertise now, while the technology stands on the threshold of useful applications.
Moderna and IBM researchers used a financial risk assessment method called Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) to
boost how well Iterational Quantum Algorithms (IQA) work.
While IBM works to suppress noise at the hardware level—with improved architectures such as the IBM
Quantum® Heron processor that offers lower error rates—additional error mitigation techniques are still often
required.
Quantum-centric supercomputing works to divide problems between quantum and classical architectures, with each
augmenting the other’s ability to rapidly deliver results to once unsolvable problems. The IBM–Moderna team is
focused on quantum-centric approaches to the secondary structure problem at even larger scales.
E.ON uses quantum computing to help tackle complexity
As one of Europe’s largest energy companies, E.ON operates a 1.6-million-kilometer energy network to serve 47
million customers with electricity and gas across 17 European countries. E.ON powers homes, hospitals, factories,
transit and other critical infrastructure essential to 21st-century life.
Now power comes from smaller, more dynamic sources, including solar and wind. At the same time, electric vehicles
and intelligent systems in homes are dramatically changing consumption patterns, and more sectors are becoming
electrified, such as mobility and heating. E.ON, responsible for providing power 24x7 year round, has to prepare well
in advance to account for complex dynamic changes in both supply and demand. The company is now exploring
quantum computing, a tool for conquering complexity, as a solution to this problem.
The team utilized an advanced IBM Qiskit capability—dynamic circuits—to simplify a very large problem. This let
them successfully tackle a challenge that was too big for an earlier 27-qubit IBM Quantum computer.
Mercedes-Benz bets on quantum to craft the future of electric vehicles
Modern lithium-ion batteries are a key to a sustainable energy future, but the next major advance could be the
lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery, which promises a dramatic leap in performance, including double to quadruple capacity
and lower costs. Developing this new chemistry is a complex and lengthy research challenge, making molecular
simulation an ideal approach before physical prototyping. However, simulating even simple molecules like caffeine,
let alone complex battery materials, overwhelms the power of classical supercomputers due to the enormous
number of quantum states. A Mercedes-Benz and Daimler Trucks {R & D} team, in collaboration with IBM, used a
quantum-inspired computing method to simulate the stability of new compounds. This foundational work aims to
accelerate the development of Li-S batteries and aligns with Mercedes-Benz's goal of achieving a carbon-neutral car
fleet and having over 50% electric/hybrid sales by 2030.
Boeing seeks new ways to engineer strong, lightweight materials
Problem: Designing super-strong, lightweight ply composites for airplanes involves optimization problems with
10,000 to 100,000 variables, which are too complex for today's classical computers to solve whole.
Current Solution: Boeing breaks these problems into smaller, manageable pieces for classical computers, then
pieces the results back together, which can be less efficient for manufacturing long, straight composite lines.
Quantum Goal: Boeing hopes quantum computers can solve the whole problem at once, leading to more efficient
designs and manufacturing processes.
Collaboration & Innovation: Boeing and IBM Quantum created a cutdown ply composite problem (40 variables) as a
test case. They developed a new quantum optimization approach that efficiently encoded three binary variables per
qubit instead of the typical one.
Result: This innovation allowed them to successfully solve the problem on a real IBM quantum computer, nearly
doubling the previous record for the largest binary optimization problem solved by quantum hardware at the time,
demonstrating the potential of quantum methods for a core Boeing design challenge.
Future Outlook: While several years of work are still needed for quantum computers to scale up and be fully
integrated into Boeing's design process, this success has accelerated their progress and built Boeing's internal
quantum expertise.

The advent of quantum computing technology promises a revolution in computational power, capable of solving
complex problems that were previously deemed intractable. However, as with any ground-breaking technology, the
development and deployment of quantum computing come with a countless of ethical and societal implications
that must be carefully considered. From privacy concerns to geopolitical consequences and accessibility issues,
stakeholders must navigate a complex landscape to ensure that quantum computing advances benefit humanity
while minimizing potential harms.
Ethical Considerations in Quantum Computing
The development and deployment of quantum computing technology raise several ethical considerations:
Data Privacy and Security: Quantum computers could potentially break many current encryption methods,
threatening data privacy and security. On the other hand, they could also lead to the development of new, ultra-
secure cryptographic techniques.
Fair Access and Digital Divide: As quantum computers are currently expensive and require specialized knowledge to
operate, there’s a risk of widening the digital divide. Ensuring fair access to quantum computing resources is a
significant ethical consideration.
Regulation and Oversight: The rapid advancement of quantum computing technology may outpace the
development of regulations and oversight mechanisms. Balancing innovation with ethical considerations requires
careful thought and proactive policy-making.
Environmental Impact: Quantum computers require low temperatures to operate, which can lead to high energy
consumption. The environmental impact of large-scale deployment of quantum computers is an important
consideration.
Privacy Concerns:
Privacy concerns appear large in the era of quantum computing.
With exponentially increased processing power, quantum computers have the potential to break current encryption
standards, endangering the security of sensitive data such as financial records, personal communications, and
government secrets.
This raises profound ethical questions about the balance between privacy and security in the digital age.
As quantum computing capabilities continue to evolve, robust encryption methods must be developed to safeguard
privacy rights in a quantum-powered world.
Additionally, regulations and oversight mechanisms must be put in place to prevent misuse of quantum computing
technology for intrusive surveillance or malicious cyber activities.
Moreover, policymakers must deal with questions of data ownership, consent, and transparency to protect
individuals' privacy rights in a quantum-enabled world.
Challenges to adoption: cost, skills, standardization
Cost and Accessibility:
High Costs: Building quantum computers requires significant investment in specialized, cutting-edge hardware,
including specialized materials, complex control circuits, and advanced cooling technologies. Superconducting qubits,
for instance, must be kept at temperatures near absolute zero using expensive and complex dilution refrigerators.
Limited Availability: Currently, access to quantum hardware is primarily limited to large corporations, governments,
and research institutions, hindering wider experimentation and adoption by smaller organizations. Cloud-based
quantum computing services are emerging to address this, but costs remain a barrier.
Workforce and Expertise:
Talent Shortage: There is a significant shortage of professionals with the interdisciplinary skills required for quantum
computing, including expertise in quantum mechanics, computer science, and engineering.
Specialized knowledge: Quantum computing demands a deep understanding of quantum mechanics, linear algebra,
and advanced computer science. This interdisciplinary skill set is rare outside of academic and research
environments.
Lack of practical training: The programming paradigms for quantum computers are fundamentally different from
classical computers, and the lack of development, user-friendly software tools complicates the learning curve.
Standardization and Bench Marking:
Hardware and software incompatibility: The industry is home to numerous competing qubit technologies, such as
superconducting circuits, trapped ions, and photonic systems. The resulting diversity of hardware architectures
means software and algorithms created for one platform may not be easily portable to another.
Inconsistent performance metrics: Without common benchmarks, it is difficult for users to objectively compare the
performance and capabilities of different quantum systems.
Timing of standards: Establishing standards too early risks stifling innovation by locking in immature technology.
However, waiting too long could lead to market fragmentation and vendor lock-in. Organizations like
the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) are working to determine the right time and approach for
developing these critical guidelines.
Security protocols: As quantum computing advances, it poses a threat to current cryptographic methods. The
transition to post-quantum cryptography requires new standards to ensure data remains secure, a complex and
time-consuming process.
Defining Benchmarks: Developing meaningful benchmarks to evaluate the performance and capabilities of quantum
computers for specific applications is an ongoing challenge.
Emerging careers in quantum: roles, skillsets, and preparation pathways
Who Can Pursue a Career in Quantum Computing?
Quantum computing is highly interdisciplinary, so students from various science backgrounds can enter the field:
Physics: Ideal for those interested in quantum mechanics and hardware development.
Computer Science: Perfect for those focusing on quantum algorithms and software.
Engineering: Useful for designing quantum processors and experimental setups.
Mathematics: Essential for algorithm design, cryptography, and modeling quantum systems.
For example, a physicist may work on building quantum chips, while a computer science student might develop
software that runs on these chips. The field values diverse expertise and collaborative problem-solving.
Key Skills Required
1. Technical Skills
To succeed in quantum computing, students should focus on these core technical areas:
Quantum programming languages: Qiskit, Cirq, and other frameworks for building quantum algorithms.
Mathematics: Linear algebra, probability, and complex numbers are crucial.
Quantum mechanics principles: Superposition, entanglement, and decoherence.
2. Soft Skills
In addition to technical knowledge, soft skills are equally important:
Analytical thinking: Ability to solve complex problems efficiently.
Collaboration: Working in interdisciplinary teams is common.
Adaptability: The field evolves quickly, requiring a continuous learning mindset.
Job Roles and Opportunities
Quantum computing offers a variety of career paths across research, development, and industry applications:
Quantum Research Scientist: Focuses on theoretical or experimental quantum studies.
Quantum Software Developer: Designs algorithms and programs for quantum computers.
Quantum Hardware Engineer: Builds and tests quantum processors.
Industry Roles: Cryptography, AI, pharmaceuticals, and finance sectors are increasingly hiring quantum specialists.
Companies like IBM, Google, Microsoft, and emerging startups offer internships and full-time positions for graduates
entering this field. Fresh graduates can often start in research labs or software development teams and gradually
specialize in quantum algorithms or hardware design.
Educational Pathways
A structured educational pathway can significantly improve your chances of success:
Undergraduate Courses: Physics, computer science, mathematics, and electrical engineering.
Master’s and PhD Programs: Specialized degrees in quantum computing, quantum information, or quantum
engineering.
Online Certifications & Workshops: Platforms like MITx, Coursera, and Qiskit offer practical exposure.
For example, a student pursuing a master’s in quantum computing can simultaneously contribute to open-source
projects, gaining real-world experience while completing their degree.
Emerging Trends in the Quantum Computing Market in India
India is rapidly growing in terms of emerging trends in the quantum computing market worldwide. Backed by
governmental support, private sector investments, and powerful academic and research institutions, the country
aims to forefront innovations in the quantum technologies field. The Indian research and commercial quantum
computing sectors are flourishing. These trends are creating new opportunities across various industries, ranging
from information technology to healthcare, and reshaping the technological development landscape in the country.
• Government Support and Initiatives: The Indian government has significantly increased its focus on quantum
technologies. Programs like the National Mission on Quantum Technologies and Applications (NMQTA) aim to
provide strategic direction and funding for quantum research. The government has accelerated the country’s
quantum computing capabilities with an investment of INR 8,000 crore in quantum technologies. This encourages
public-private collaboration and pushes India to become a better participant in the quantum race by setting a
framework for innovation and infrastructure development.
• Collaboration Between Academia and Industry: In India, academia and industry are increasingly collaborating on
quantum computing projects. Leading institutions like IITs (Indian Institutes of Technology), IISc (Indian Institute of
Science), and government research bodies such as ISRO and DRDO are partnering with tech companies to drive
innovation in quantum computing. These collaborations are crucial for accelerating research and translating
quantum theories into practical, commercial applications. As a result, India is becoming a hub for quantum
computing research and development, with promising prospects for commercializing quantum technologies.
