Text Representation and Statistical NLP
Text Representation in NLP
Text must be converted into numerical form so that machine learning models can process it. This transformation is called text representation.
There are two major approaches:
Bag of Words (BoW)
Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF)
Bag of Words (BoW)
The Bag of Words (BoW) model is a simple and widely used technique to represent text as numerical vectors. The core idea is:
We treat a text document as a "bag" of its words, ignoring grammar and word order.
We represent each document by the frequency of words occurring in it.
This is a text-to-vector conversion technique, used as input to machine learning models.
Step-by-Step Explanation
Step 1: Vocabulary Building
Collect all unique words across the document(s).
This set of unique words becomes the vocabulary.
For example, for 3 documents:
Doc1: "I love NLP"
Doc2: "I love Machine Learning"
Doc3: "NLP is fun"
Vocabulary: [I, love, NLP, Machine, Learning, is, fun]
Step-by-Step Explanation
Step 2: Vectorization
Each document is converted into a vector of counts.
The length of each vector = size of vocabulary.
Each position in the vector represents a word from the vocabulary and its value is the count of that word in the document.
So:
Doc1 → [1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0]
Doc2 → [1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0]
Doc3 → [0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1]
This is your BoW matrix, also called document-term matrix.
Advantages of BoW
Simple to understand and implement.
Works well for small and medium-sized corpora.
Good baseline for classification tasks.
Limitations of BoW
No understanding of meaning: The model doesn't understand that “good” and “excellent” are similar.
No word order: "I love NLP" and "NLP love I" will be treated the same.
High dimensionality: Vocabulary can become very large for large datasets.
Sparsity: Most entries in the vector are 0.
Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF)
BoW only counts how many times a word appears, but treats all words as equally important.
TF-IDF addresses this limitation by:
Highlighting important words in a document.
Down-weighting common words (like "the", "is", "and") that appear in almost every document.
TF-IDF = TF × IDF
TF (Term Frequency)
It tells how frequently a term appears in a document.
For example, if “NLP” appears 3 times in a document of 100 words:
IDF (Inverse Document Frequency)
It tells how rare or unique a word is across all documents.
Where:
N = Total number of documents
df(t) = Number of documents containing the term t
If a term appears in every document, df(t)=N, so:
Meaning: very common words get zero weight.
TF-IDF Weight
Multiply the TF and IDF:
TF-IDF(t,d)=TF(t,d)×IDF(t)
This gives a high score to terms that:
Appear frequently in a particular document
Appear rarely in other documents
Example
Suppose you have 3 documents:
Doc1: "AI is our future"
Doc2: "AI and ML are part of future"
Doc3: "ML and DL are trends in AI"
Let’s say the word "AI" appears in all 3 documents.
TF of "AI" in Doc1 = 1/4 = 0.25
IDF of "AI" = log(3/3) = 0
So TF-IDF("AI", Doc1) = 0.25 × 0 = 0
Meaning: "AI" is not a distinguishing term, so it gets zero importance.
Advantages of TF-IDF
Balances term frequency and uniqueness.
Improves over raw BoW by reducing weights of common words.
Widely used in search engines, document classification, and recommendation.
Limitations of TF-IDF
Still ignores semantic meaning (e.g., “car” and “automobile” are treated as unrelated).
Doesn’t consider word position or context.
Not suitable for capturing long-range dependencies or word meanings — that’s where word embeddings help (Word2Vec, GloVe, etc.).
Language Modeling
What is a Language Model?
A Language Model (LM) is a statistical or probabilistic model that assigns a likelihood (probability) to a sequence of words.
Purpose:
To predict the next word given the previous words.
To estimate the probability of a sentence being valid in a language.
P(w1,w2,w3,...,wn)
Where w1,w2,...,wn​ are words in a sequence.
Language models are the foundation of NLP tasks like:
Machine Translation
Speech Recognition
Text Generation
Spell Correction
Unigrams
A unigram is a single word. A unigram model assumes that each word is independent of the previous one.
In simpler terms, the probability of a sentence is computed by multiplying the individual probabilities of each word, assuming no context.

Unigram Model:
P(“I”)×P(“love”)×P(“NLP”)
This model is easy to compute and works well when context is not important.

N-gram Models
An N-gram model generalizes unigrams and bigrams by using N-1 words of context to predict the next word.
Unigram: N=1 (no context)
Bigram: N=2 (1-word context)
Trigram: N=3 (2-word context)
4-gram: N=4 (3-word context)
And so on…
Example
Sentence: "I love NLP very much"
Trigram Model:
P(“I”) × P(“love” ∣ “I” ) × P(“NLP” ∣ “I”, “love”) × P(“very” ∣ “love”, “NLP”) × P(“much” ∣ “NLP”, “very”)
The higher the value of N, the more context the model uses, and typically the better it can predict.

More accurate than unigram or bigram.

Word Embeddings
Word Embeddings in NLP are a way to represent words as numeric vectors (arrays of numbers) so that computers can understand and process them.
Why do we need Word Embeddings?
Computers can't understand text directly—they understand numbers.
So we need to convert words into a form that machines can work with.
But we don't want just any numbers—we want numbers that capture the meaning of words.
What are Word Embeddings?
Word embeddings are dense vector representations of words in a continuous vector space where:
Similar words are placed closer together.
Dissimilar words are placed farther apart.
Example
How are they created?
They are usually learned from large text datasets using neural networks. Common methods include:
Word2Vec
GloVe (Global Vectors)
FastText
Example of use
If you're building a chatbot or sentiment analyzer, word embeddings help the model understand:
“awesome” and “great” mean similar things.
“hate” and “dislike” are negative.
Word2Vec
Word2Vec is a method to convert words into vectors such that words used in similar contexts have similar vectors.
It uses a neural network to learn these word representations from large text data.
Two Main Models in Word2Vec
CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words):
Predicts a word based on its surrounding context.
Example:
For the sentence:
“The cat sat on the mat”
If the context is: [The, cat, on, the, mat],the model tries to predict the center word: "sat".
Skip-Gram:
Does the opposite: predicts surrounding context words given the center word.
From the same sentence:
If the center word is "sat", the model tries to predict [The, cat, on, the, mat].
CBOW
CBOW is a model in Word2Vec that learns to predict a target word from its context words.
Context = words around a target word.
It helps the model learn which words tend to appear together.
CBOW Process
For a given sentence, the model:
Takes context words (before and after a word).
Predicts the target (center) word.
Sample Sentence
Example: "The cat sat on the mat"
Suppose we choose window size = 2, which means 2 words on each side are context.
Let’s say our target word is "sat".
Then, the context words are:
"The", "cat", "on", "the"
CBOW Input and Output:
The model learns from many such examples and adjusts word vectors so that words in similar contexts end up with similar vector representations.
Skip-Gram
Skip-Gram is the opposite of CBOW.
Instead of predicting the target word from its context words (like CBOW),
Skip-Gram uses the target word to predict its context words.
Intuition Behind Skip-Gram
Let’s say you have the sentence:
"The cat sat on the mat"
Imagine you focus on the word "sat".
Skip-Gram will try to predict the words around "sat" — like “cat”, “on”, “the”
Simple Example Sentence
"The cat sits on the mat"
Let’s take the target word as "cat", and a window size of 2 (means 2 words before and after the target).
Context Window
Target word: "cat"
Context words: ["The", "sits"]
So, Skip-Gram pairs would be:
("cat", "The") ("cat", "sits")
How Skip-Gram Works
For each word in the sentence, it generates such (target, context) pairs. For example:
For "sits": context words = ["cat", "on"] →→ pairs: ("sits", "cat"), ("sits", "on")
For "on": context words = ["sits", "the"] →→ pairs: ("on", "sits"), ("on", "the")
What it Learns?
By seeing many such pairs, the Skip-Gram model learns vector representations of words such that words appearing in similar contexts will have similar vectors.
Intuition in Layman's Terms
Imagine you are learning a new language by looking at how words appear near each other.
If you often see "doctor" near "hospital", "nurse", "patient", the model learns that "doctor" is related to those words — without knowing the meaning explicitly.
GloVe(Global Vectors for Word Representation)
What is GloVe?
GloVe is a word embedding technique developed by Stanford.
Unlike Word2Vec (which learns embeddings from predicting context), GloVe uses global word co-occurrence statistics to capture the meaning of words.
Goal:
To map words into a continuous vector space where semantically similar words are close together.
Key Intuition Behind GloVe
Let’s say the word “ice” occurs often with “cold”, and “steam” occurs with “hot”, but rarely do “ice” and “steam” co-occur.This pattern tells us something about how these words are related.
GloVe builds word vectors in such a way that these co-occurrence patterns are encoded into the geometry of the vector space.

Step 2: Learn Vectors
GloVe trains a model so that:
Dot product of word vector w_i and context vector w_j ≈ log(co-occurrence count)
This means GloVe tries to find word vectors such that:
king • man ≈ log(20)
queen • woman ≈ log(15)
And so on.

Which shows that:
“king” is to “man” as “queen” is to “woman”
It captures semantic relationships in vector space
Why is GloVe Powerful?
Combines global statistics (co-occurrence) with the vector representation power.
Learns linear substructures (e.g., king - man + woman ≈ queen)
Performs better on many NLP benchmarks than Word2Vec.
FastText – Word Embedding Method
FastText is an extension of Word2Vec, developed by Facebook AI. Unlike Word2Vec, which treats each word as a single unit, FastText represents a word as a bag of character n-grams.
This allows FastText to:
Understand morphology (structure of words)
Handle rare words or even misspellings
Better generalize to out-of-vocabulary words
Why FastText?
Let’s say we have two words:
“playing”
“played”
Word2Vec learns separate vectors for these. It has no idea they're related.
But FastText breaks words into subwords (like “play”, “ing”, “pla”, “lay”, etc.) and learns vectors for those too.
FastText with a Simple Example
Let’s take the word:
"playing"
Assume we use character 3-grams (trigrams).
FastText represents "playing" as:
<pl, pla, lay, ayi, yin, ing, ng>
It also includes the full word "playing" in the vector.
Example Trigrams:
<pl, pla, lay, ayi, yin, ing, ng>
< and > are special characters to mark beginning and end.
How It Learns
Instead of: vector("playing")
FastText does:
vector("playing") = average(vector("pla"), vector("lay"), ..., vector("ing"))
So if a new word like "playment" comes, which wasn’t in training:
FastText can still create a meaningful vector using its subword parts.
Why It’s Powerful
All these words share common subword n-grams — so FastText gives them similar embeddings, capturing their relationship.
Advantage
Works well on:
Low-resource languages
Misspellings (e.g., “happi” vs “happy”)
Morphologically rich languages like German, Tamil, etc.
Handles rare or unseen words
Captures word structure (prefixes, suffixes)
Conclusion
FastText = Word2Vec + Subword information
It learns word meanings by breaking words into character n-grams and combining their vectors.

Text Classification using Naive Bayes and SVM
Text classification is a process of assigning categories (like spam or not spam, positive or negative) to text data.
Two popular machine learning algorithms used for this are Naive Bayes and Support Vector Machine (SVM).
Naive Bayes Classifier
Naive Bayes is a probabilistic machine learning algorithm based on Bayes' Theorem.
It is called "naive" because it assumes that all features (words) are independent of each other given the class label — which is often not true in real language, but the model still performs surprisingly well in practice.

Steps in Naive Bayes for Text Classification
Preprocess Text: Tokenize, remove stop words, and convert text to a numerical representation (e.g., Bag of Words).
Calculate Priors: Compute P(C), the probability of each class based on training data.
Calculate Likelihoods: Estimate P(wi​∣C), the probability of word wi appearing in class C, using word frequencies.
Classify: For a new document, compute the posterior probability for each class and choose the class with the highest probability.

Support Vector Machine (SVM)
Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a supervised machine learning algorithm used for classification tasks, including in Natural Language Processing (NLP) for problems like text classification (e.g., spam detection, sentiment analysis).
SVM aims to find the optimal hyperplane that best separates data points of different classes in a high-dimensional space, maximizing the margin (distance) between the hyperplane and the nearest data points (support vectors).
For non-linearly separable data, SVM uses a "kernel trick" to transform the data into a higher-dimensional space where a linear boundary can be found.
Key Concepts
Hyperplane: A decision boundary separating classes (e.g., a line in 2D, a plane in 3D).
Margin: The distance between the hyperplane and the nearest data point. SVM maximizes this margin for better generalization.
Support Vectors: The data points closest to the hyperplane, which define the margin.
Kernel Trick: For non-linear data, SVM applies kernels (e.g., linear, polynomial, or Radial Basis Function (RBF)) to map data to a higher-dimensional space.
Soft Margin: Allows some misclassifications (controlled by a parameter C) to handle noisy data.
Application in NLP: SVM is effective for text classification due to its ability to handle high-dimensional, sparse data (e.g., Bag of Words or TF-IDF vectors).
Steps in SVM for Text Classification
Preprocess Text: Convert text to numerical features (e.g., Bag of Words, TF-IDF).
Train SVM: Find the hyperplane that maximizes the margin between classes using training data.
Classify: Use the trained model to predict the class of new text based on its feature vector.

1. Accuracy
While accuracy provides a quick snapshot, it can be misleading in cases of imbalanced datasets.
For example, in a dataset with 90% class A and 10% class B, a model predicting only class A will still achieve 90% accuracy but it will fail to identify any class B instances.
Accuracy is good but it gives a False Positive sense of achieving high accuracy. The problem arises due to the possibility of misclassification of minor class samples being very high.
2. Precision
It measures how many of the positive predictions made by the model are actually correct.
It's useful when the cost of false positives is high such as in medical diagnoses where predicting a disease when it’s not present can have serious consequences.
2. Precision
Precision helps ensure that when the model predicts a positive outcome, it’s likely to be correct.
Interpretation: Out of all emails predicted as spam, 88.8% were actually spam.
Useful if we want to avoid wrongly flagging legitimate emails.
3. Recall or true positive rate
Recall or Sensitivity measures how many of the actual positive cases were correctly identified by the model.
It is important when missing a positive case (false negative) is more costly than false positives.
3. Recall or true positive rate
Interpretation: The model caught 80% of the spam emails.
Useful if we want to make sure we detect most spam.
In scenarios where catching all positive cases is important (like disease detection), recall is a key metric.
4. F1-Score
The F1 Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.
It is useful when we need a balance between precision and recall as it combines both into a single number.
A high F1 score means the model performs well on both metrics. Its range is [0,1].
Lower recall and higher precision gives us great accuracy but then it misses a large number of instances.
More the F1 score better will be performance.
