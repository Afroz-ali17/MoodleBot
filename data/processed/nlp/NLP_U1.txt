Fundamentals of Natural Language Processing
What is Natural Language Processing?
Natural Language Processing is a part of artificial intelligence that aims to teach the human language with all its complexities to computers.
This is so that machines can understand and interpret the human language to eventually understand human communication in a better way.
Natural Language Processing is a cross among many different fields such as artificial intelligence, computational linguistics, human-computer interaction, etc.
There are many different methods in NLP to understand human language which include statistical and machine learning methods.
Why is Natural Language Processing important?
It allows computers to understand human language and then analyze huge amounts of language-based data in an unbiased way.
This is the reason that Natural Language Processing has many diverse applications these days in fields ranging from IT to telecommunications to academics.

Challenges of Natural Language Processing (NLP)
Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a field of study focused on designing methods and algorithms that can handle unstructured human language data as input or output.
Understanding and generating human language using machines is a highly challenging task.
This complexity arises because human language is inherently ambiguous, highly variable, and constantly evolving.
Challenges of Natural Language Processing (NLP)
For example, the sentence "I ate pizza with friends" can have a similar structure to "I ate pizza with olives", but the meaning changes significantly.
Likewise, the same message can be conveyed in various ways, such as “I ate pizza with friends” and “Friends and I shared some pizza”, highlighting the variability of expression.
1. Ambiguous
A sentence, phrase, or word is ambiguous when it has more than one possible meaning.
Example:
“I saw the man with the telescope.”
Did I use the telescope to see the man?
Or does the man have the telescope?
“Bank”
Can mean river bank or financial bank.
Why it’s a challenge:
Humans use context and common sense to resolve ambiguity.
Computers don’t understand context naturally—they need special algorithms to guess the correct meaning.
Analogy:
It’s like reading a sentence with missing puzzle pieces. You can guess it, but a computer might get the wrong picture.
2. Highly Variable
There are many different ways to say the same thing in natural language.
Example:
“I’m hungry.”
“I could use something to eat.”
“My stomach is growling.”
“Let’s grab a bite.”
All mean the same thing, but are expressed very differently.
Why it’s a challenge:
NLP systems must understand that different wordings may mean the same.
This requires semantic understanding, which is hard for traditional rule-based systems.
Analogy:
It’s like trying to recognize the same song when played with different instruments, speed, or singers.
3. Constantly Evolving
Language keeps changing over time—new words, new meanings, new ways of speaking keep appearing.
Example:
“Google” used to be just a company name. Now it means “to search online.”
Words like “selfie,” “emoji,” “hashtag,” “ghosting” didn’t exist 20 years ago.
Slang changes often:
“Cool” → “Lit” → “Fire” → “Slaps” (for something good)
Why it’s a challenge:
NLP models trained today may not understand words invented next year.
Keeping models updated with current language use is essential.
Analogy:
Imagine learning a game whose rules change every week—it’s hard to master.

In addition to ambiguity and variability, natural language exhibits three other properties that further complicate its computational processing:
Discreteness
Compositionality
Sparsity
1. Discreteness
Language is symbolic and discrete, meaning that its basic units—such as characters and words—are individual symbols with meanings that are not inherent to the symbols themselves.
For instance, the words “hamburger” and “pizza” are completely distinct symbols, and there is no way to infer their meaning or relationship just from the characters or structure.
This contrasts with domains like image processing or audio signals, where the data is continuous and allows smooth transformations and comparisons.
2. Compositionality
Language is compositional, meaning that smaller units combine to form larger, meaningful structures.
Letters form words, words form phrases and sentences, and sentences convey complex meanings that go beyond the sum of individual word meanings.
To interpret natural language, one must consider these higher-level structures and their interactions.
3. Sparsity
Natural language is sparse. Because words are discrete and can be combined in nearly infinite ways, the number of possible valid sentences is extremely large.
Most sentences we encounter are ones we have never seen before. Even short sequences of words in books or newspapers are often novel.
This makes generalization difficult, as there is no straightforward way to relate new sentences to those in the training data unless the model captures deep meaning representations—which are not directly observable.
This issue persists even with large datasets: rare and unseen combinations are always present.
1. Discreteness
Natural language is discrete, meaning it consists of distinct, separate units like characters, words, or tokens—not continuous signals.
Example: Think of the words: "cat", "bat", "rat"
They differ by only one letter, but for a computer, they are completely different tokens or word IDs. There is no built-in relationship between them unless we explicitly teach the model (e.g., using embeddings like Word2Vec or BERT).
Why this is a challenge:
In images, pixels change gradually, so small changes don’t matter much.
But in language, changing just one word or letter can change the meaning entirely.
Computers need to treat each word as a unique item, and this makes learning harder unless we map them to vector spaces (like in word embeddings).
Analogy:
Imagine a library where every book has a completely random code. Without a catalog, it’s impossible to know which books are related—this is how a computer feels about discrete language.
2. Compositionality
The meaning of a sentence is not just the sum of the word meanings—it depends on how the words are combined or ordered.
Example:
"The dog chased the cat."
"The cat chased the dog."
"Chased the dog the cat."
All of these contain the same words, but: The first and second sentences mean opposite things. The third is grammatically incorrect and confusing.
Why this is a challenge:
Computers need to understand grammar, syntax, and word order to grasp meaning.
Just recognizing the words isn’t enough; the structure (who is doing what to whom) matters.
In languages with flexible word order (like Hindi or German), this becomes even harder.
Analogy:
Imagine building a house using blocks. The same blocks can create a different shape depending on how they’re arranged.
3. Sparsity
There are billions of possible valid sentences, but only a small number of them are used often or present in datasets.
Example:
You might say: "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog."
But a rare sentence like: "The purple dinosaur danced under Saturn’s rings" is unusual, but still grammatically valid.
Why this is a challenge:
A language model might never see some valid phrases in training.
This makes it hard to predict or understand new combinations, even though they make sense.
It also increases the data requirement—we need lots of diverse examples to help models generalize.
Analogy:
Imagine trying to learn cooking from only five recipes. When given a new ingredient combination, you wouldn’t know what to do.
Linguistic Essentials
In the field of Natural Language Processing (NLP), it is essential to understand how human language is structured and interpreted.
Linguistics provides the foundation for this understanding.
Three fundamental branches of linguistics that play a vital role in NLP are syntax, semantics, and pragmatics.
Each of these areas contributes to different layers of language analysis, from sentence structure to meaning and context.
1. Syntax
Syntax refers to the set of rules that determine the structure of sentences in a language.
It focuses on how words are arranged to form grammatically correct phrases and sentences.
For instance, in English, the typical word order is subject-verb-object, as in “She reads a book.” If this order is disrupted, as in “Reads she a book,” the sentence becomes syntactically incorrect.
In NLP, syntax is important for tasks such as part-of-speech tagging and parsing, where the goal is to understand the grammatical structure of sentences.
Tools like syntactic parsers help break down sentences into hierarchical structures, which are useful for deeper language processing.
2. Semantics
Semantics is concerned with the meaning of words, phrases, and sentences. It explores how language conveys information and what specific expressions mean in a literal sense.
For example, the sentence “The cat sat on the mat” conveys a clear and direct meaning. However, semantics can also deal with ambiguity and figurative language.
Consider the sentence “He kicked the bucket”; literally, it may seem like a physical action, but semantically, it could also mean that someone has died.
In NLP, semantic analysis is used in machine translation, information retrieval, and word embedding techniques such as Word2Vec and GloVe, which represent words based on their meanings and relationships with other words.
3. Pragmatics
Pragmatics extends beyond literal meanings and focuses on how language is used in real-world contexts.
It considers the speaker’s intention, the relationship between speakers, and the situational context to interpret meaning accurately.
For instance, when someone says, “Can you pass the salt?” they are not questioning your ability to pass salt but politely requesting that you do so.
Pragmatics plays a crucial role in NLP applications like chatbots, sentiment analysis, and discourse understanding, where the system must infer implied meanings, handle indirect speech, and respond appropriately to users.
Linguistic Essentials
Together, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics form the core linguistic foundations that enable machines to process, understand, and generate human language effectively.
A good NLP system must account for all three aspects to interpret language in a way that closely mirrors human understanding.
Summary
Syntax: Syntax deals with the structure and grammatical rules of a language. It helps NLP systems analyze how words are arranged to form sentences. For example, parsing techniques use syntactic rules to identify noun phrases, verb phrases, and sentence constituents.
Semantics: Semantics is concerned with the meaning of words, phrases, and sentences. It allows machines to understand what is being said, beyond just structure. Techniques like word embeddings and semantic role labeling are used to capture meaning in NLP.
Pragmatics: Pragmatics focuses on the context in which language is used. It interprets the speaker’s intention, implied meanings, and how meaning can change depending on the situation. Pragmatic analysis helps resolve ambiguity and understand indirect speech acts in NLP applications like chatbots and virtual assistants.
Text Processing
Text processing is the foundational step in NLP, where raw and unstructured textual data is transformed into a structured and analyzable format.
Since human language is inherently messy—filled with variations, ambiguities, grammatical inconsistencies, and noise—it is essential to clean and standardize it before any meaningful analysis or modeling can be done.
The overall goal of text processing is to prepare the text so that computational algorithms can understand and analyze it effectively.
This preparation typically involves two major phases: text preprocessing and linguistic analysis.
Text Preprocessing
The first phase, text preprocessing, includes several sequential operations such as
Tokenization
Normalization
Stopword Removal
Stemming
Lemmatization
N-gram generation
POS Tagging
Named Entity Recognition
These steps aim to reduce the noise in the text, simplify linguistic structures, and make the text uniform and manageable for downstream tasks.
1. Tokenization
Tokenization is the process of splitting a sentence or paragraph into smaller units called tokens.
These tokens can be words, characters, or subwords, depending on the application.
For example, the sentence “NLP is fascinating” would be tokenized into ["NLP", "is", "fascinating"].
Tokenization helps to separate text into meaningful components and is typically the first step in text processing.
Without tokenization, it is not possible to analyze or compute upon the text effectively.
2. Normalization
Normalization is the process of bringing all text to a standard and consistent format.
This includes converting all text to lowercase, removing punctuation marks, eliminating extra spaces, converting accented characters (like café to cafe), and handling other irregularities.
For instance, the sentence " NLP! is Fun. " would be normalized to "nlp is fun".
This process ensures that minor differences in how text is written do not affect the outcome of analysis or modeling.
3. Stopword Removal
In natural language, many words occur frequently but do not carry significant meaning or contribute much to the analysis.
These are known as stopwords and include words such as “the”, “is”, “in”, “and”, “a”, etc.
Removing stopwords helps to reduce the noise in the data and allows the focus to remain on the words that are more meaningful for the given task.
For example, in the sentence "The dog is on the mat", removing stopwords results in ["dog", "mat"], which still conveys the core idea.
However, this step must be used carefully in contexts like sentiment analysis, where even a stopword like “not” could reverse the sentiment of a sentence.
4. Stemming
Stemming also deals with word reduction, but it does so using crude rules that strip prefixes or suffixes to arrive at a root form.
It does not consider whether the resulting root word is valid or meaningful.
For example, "playing", "played", and "plays" are all reduced to "play".
Sometimes, stemming may produce incorrect root forms like converting "universities" to "univers".
While it is faster than lemmatization, it is also less accurate.
It is often used in search engines or text classification tasks where precision in meaning is not critical.
5. Lemmatization
Lemmatization refers to the process of reducing words to their base or dictionary form, known as a lemma.
Unlike stemming, lemmatization takes into account the grammatical structure and context of a word.
For instance, words like "am", "is", and "are" are all lemmatized to "be".
Similarly, "running", "ran", and "runs" are converted to "run".
This process is based on vocabulary and morphological analysis and ensures that different forms of the same word are treated as a single entity in further analysis.
6. N-gram Generation
Sometimes understanding individual words is not sufficient because context matters in human language.
N-gram generation helps address this by creating a sequence of N contiguous words.
For example, from the sentence "NLP is fun", the 2-grams (or bigrams) are ["NLP is", "is fun"].
N-grams are especially useful in tasks such as language modeling, text prediction, and spam detection where the relationship between adjacent words provides meaningful insights.
7. Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging
Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging is a fundamental step in text processing where each word in a sentence is labeled with its grammatical category, such as noun, verb, adjective, etc.
This helps machines understand the syntactic structure of language, which is crucial for tasks like parsing, translation, and question answering.
POS tagging can be performed using rule-based systems, statistical models, or machine learning approaches.
For example, in the sentence “The cat sat on the mat,” each word is tagged according to its role in the sentence (e.g., “cat” as a noun, “sat” as a verb).
8. Named Entity Recognition (NER)
Named Entity Recognition (NER) is the process of identifying and classifying named entities in text into categories like person names, locations, organizations, dates, and more.
It adds semantic understanding by extracting real-world entities from unstructured text, enabling information extraction and content summarization.
NER is commonly used in applications like search engines and digital assistants.
For instance, in the sentence “Apple was founded by Steve Jobs in California,” NER identifies “Apple” as an organization, “Steve Jobs” as a person, and “California” as a location.
NLP Libraries : NLTK and SpaCy Overview
In the field of Natural Language Processing (NLP), two widely used open-source libraries are NLTK (Natural Language Toolkit) and SpaCy.
Both libraries provide essential tools for text processing, but they are designed with different goals in mind and cater to slightly different audiences.
NLTK
NLTK is a comprehensive platform primarily used for teaching, research, and prototyping.
It offers access to a large collection of text corpora, lexical resources (like WordNet), and a wide range of functionalities such as tokenization, stemming, tagging, parsing, and semantic reasoning.
NLTK is often favored in academic settings because of its rich documentation, flexibility, and ease of experimenting with linguistic concepts.
However, it can be slower and less optimized for production-scale applications.
SpaCy
SpaCy is an industrial-strength NLP library that is optimized for performance and ease of use in real-world applications.
It comes with pre-trained statistical models and supports advanced tasks like part-of-speech tagging, named entity recognition (NER), dependency parsing, and word vector integration.
SpaCy is known for its speed, efficiency, and well-structured pipeline, making it suitable for deployment in production environments.
Unlike NLTK, it focuses less on educational tools and more on high-level NLP tasks and integration with modern machine learning workflows.
Summary
In summary, NLTK is ideal for learning and prototyping, while SpaCy is better suited for building fast and robust NLP applications.
Understanding both libraries allows students to choose the appropriate tool based on their project goals—whether it's experimentation or real-world deployment.
