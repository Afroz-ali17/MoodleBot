Introduction to Maintenance Strategies
• Reactive Maintenance: "Fix it when it breaks." High downtime, high
cost, potential safety hazards.
• Preventative Maintenance: Scheduled maintenance based on time or
usage (e.g., changing oil every 5,000 miles). Often results in replacing
perfectly good parts.
• Predictive Maintenance (PdM): Using data to predict exactly when a
machine will fail so maintenance can be performed just in time.
• The AI Advantage: AI finds subtle, complex patterns in multi-
dimensional sensor data that human operators and simple threshold
alarms cannot detect.
The Architecture of Predictive Maintenance
• Tier 1: Edge Devices & Sensors.
• Tier 2: Ingestion & Stream Processing. (Using tools like Apache Kafka
and Spark Streaming).
• Tier 3: The Data Lake. Historical storage of normal vs. failure states.
• Tier 4: The AI Engine. ML models continuously scoring real-time data
against historical baselines.
Data Collection – The "Vibration" Challenge
• What Data is Collected? Vibration (accelerometers), acoustics,
temperature, pressure, and oil analysis.
• The Volume Problem: A single vibration sensor sampling at 10kHz
generates massive amounts of data in hours.
• Feature Extraction: Instead of sending raw sine waves to the cloud,
edge devices often perform a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to convert
time-domain data into frequency-domain data.
Machine Learning Models for PdM
• Anomaly Detection (Unsupervised): Using algorithms like Isolation
Forests or Autoencoders to establish a "Normal Operation Baseline."
Anything outside this is flagged.
• Remaining Useful Life (RUL) Prediction (Regression): Using historical
run-to-failure data to predict exactly how many days/hours a
component has left.
• Failure Mode Classification (Supervised): Predicting which specific
part is failing (e.g., "Inner Bearing Race Defect" vs. "Misalignment").
Evaluating Predictive Maintenance Models
• Cost-Sensitive Metrics: Standard accuracy is not enough. The cost of
a False Positive (unnecessary maintenance check) is usually much
lower than the cost of a False Negative (catastrophic machine failure).
• Recall: The model must be tuned to prioritize Recall (identifying all
potential failures) over Precision.
• Alert Fatigue: If the model triggers too many False Positives,
maintenance crews will start ignoring the dashboard.
The Anatomy of Healthcare Big Data
• Unstructured Dominance: Over 80% of healthcare data is
unstructured (Medical Imaging like X-rays/MRIs, Clinical Notes,
Genomic sequences).
• The DICOM Standard: Medical images are not JPEGs. They use the
DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) format,
which contains heavy metadata (patient info, scanner calibration)
embedded with high-resolution pixel arrays.
• The Big Data Challenge: A single 3D MRI scan can be several
gigabytes. Training a model on 100,000 scans requires massive
distributed storage (HDFS/Cloud) and GPU clusters.
Deep Learning Architectures for Diagnostics
• Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs): The traditional workhorse
for medical vision. Architectures like ResNet or DenseNet excel at
learning hierarchical spatial features (edges -> textures -> complex
anatomical structures).
• Vision Transformers (ViT): The modern evolution. Instead of
convolutions, ViTs treat an image as a sequence of "patches" and use
self-attention mechanisms to understand global context across the
entire scan.
• The Compute Trade-off: ViTs often achieve superior diagnostic
accuracy but require significantly more computational power and
data to train from scratch compared to CNNs.
The Data Scarcity Problem & Transfer Learning
• The Problem: Deep learning requires millions of labeled images.
Medical datasets are small because acquiring expert annotations
(from doctors) is expensive and heavily regulated.
• The Solution: Transfer Learning. Taking a model pre-trained on a
massive dataset and "fine-tuning" its final layers on a smaller, specific
medical dataset.
• Domain-Specific Pre-training: While general datasets (like ImageNet,
containing dogs and cars) are useful, using medical-specific pre-
trained weights, such as models trained on the RadImageNet dataset,
yields vastly superior feature extraction for radiological scans.
Case Study: Automated Grading of Knee Osteoarthritis (KOA)
• The Clinical Task: Doctors use the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL)
grading system (Grades 0 to 4) to assess the severity of Knee
Osteoarthritis based on X-rays.
• The AI Pipeline:
• Preprocessing: Balancing the dataset (as severe Grade 4 cases are
rarer than healthy Grade 0 cases) using data augmentation.
• Feature Extraction: Passing the X-ray tensors through a hybrid
pipeline (e.g., combining ResNet feature maps with Vision
Transformer attention).
• Classification: Outputting a precise KL Grade, shifting the diagnosis
from a subjective human opinion to an objective mathematical
probability.
Explainable AI (XAI) in Medicine
• The "Black Box" Problem: A doctor cannot legally or ethically
prescribe surgery just because "the computer said so." They need to
know why.
• Attention Maps & Grad-CAM: Gradient-weighted Class Activation
Mapping (Grad-CAM) is used to generate visual heatmaps over the
original X-ray.
• Interpretability: The heatmap highlights the exact pixels (e.g., the
specific narrowing of the knee joint space or the presence of
osteophytes) that caused the model to output a specific diagnostic
grade.
Debugging the Human Source Code - Genomics
• Genomics is about analyzing the 'source code.‘
• Every human being is running on a base-4 operating
system written in an alphabet of just four letters: A, C, T,
and G. Your DNA is a string that is 3.2 billion characters
long.
• Twenty years ago, sequencing one human genome took
13 years and cost $3 billion. Today, Next-Generation
Sequencing (NGS) machines can do it in hours for a few
hundred dollars.
• AI, specifically distributed computing and deep learning,
steps in to find the 'bugs' in our code that lead to
disease.
Genomics as a Big Data Challenge
• The Scale of the Alphabet: A single human genome contains ~3.2
billion base pairs. Raw sequence data for one person can exceed
100GB.
• The "p > n" Problem (Curse of Dimensionality): In genomic datasets,
you often have 20,000+ features (genes, p) but only a few hundred
observations (patients, n). This makes traditional machine learning
highly prone to overfitting.
• Variety & Veracity: Genomic data is incredibly noisy. Sequencing
machines make read errors, requiring probabilistic models to
determine the true sequence.
The Computational Pipeline
• Step 1: Base Calling. Converting the raw optical signals from the
sequencing machine into A, C, T, G text strings. Deep Learning (RNNs)
is now heavily used here to improve accuracy.
• Step 2: Sequence Alignment. Taking millions of short DNA "reads"
(like shredded pieces of a book) and mapping them against a
reference human genome to reconstruct the full sequence.
• Step 3: Variant Calling. Identifying where the patient's DNA differs
from the reference genome (Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms or
SNPs).
Deep Learning for Sequence Analysis
• Predicting Gene Expression: Instead of just reading the code, AI
predicts what the code does.
• 1D Convolutional Neural Networks (1D-CNNs): While 2D-CNNs find
spatial patterns in X-rays, 1D-CNNs slide over the DNA string to find
specific "motifs" or patterns that indicate regulatory regions (like
promoters or enhancers).
• Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) / Transformers: Used to model
the sequential dependencies in DNA, understanding how a mutation
at position 100 might affect a gene at position 5,000.
Distributed Genomics with Apache Spark
• The Framework: Hail is an open-source, scalable framework built
entirely on Apache Spark, designed specifically for genomic data
analysis.
• Why Spark? Genomic matrix tables (Patients vs. Millions of genetic
variants) are too massive for Pandas or R. Spark partitions this matrix
across a cluster.
• Use Case: Performing Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) to
find the statistical correlation between specific genetic variants and a
disease (like Alzheimer's) across populations of 500,000+ people.
Pharmacogenomics (Precision Medicine)
• The Goal: Moving away from "one-size-fits-all" drug prescriptions.
• The AI Application: Training classification models that take a patient's
genetic profile as input and output the probability of an adverse drug
reaction or the predicted efficacy of a specific chemotherapy.
• Impact: Maximizing treatment success while minimizing toxic side
effects by matching the molecular signature of the tumor with the
exact chemical compound that targets it.
Patient Monitoring - The ICU at Home
• A patient is hooked up to monitors tracking ECG
(electrocardiogram), SpO2 (blood oxygen),
respiratory rate, and blood pressure continuously.
• Wearable sensors stream a patient's vitals 24/7
over Wi-Fi.
• We use Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and
Spark Streaming to not just react to a threshold, but
to predict an adverse event, like sepsis or cardiac

symptoms.
Time-Series Data in Healthcare
• Continuous Streaming: Wearables (like smartwatches, bio-patches)
generate continuous, high-frequency time-series data. An ECG sensor
might sample at 250 Hz (250 data points per second).
• The "Noise" Problem: Human movement generates massive artifacts.
If a patient brushes their teeth, the accelerometer and ECG data
become incredibly noisy.
• Temporal Dependencies: In time-series data, the sequence matters. A
heart rate of 110 is normal during exercise; a heart rate of 110 while
sleeping, preceded by a drop in oxygen, is a critical emergency.
Deep Learning for Sequence Modeling
• Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs): Networks designed with loops,
allowing information to persist. They are the standard for sequential
data.
• LSTMs (Long Short-Term Memory): A specialized RNN that solves the
"vanishing gradient" problem, allowing the model to remember
important physiological events from minutes or hours ago (e.g., a
previous skipped heartbeat).
• 1D-CNNs: Just as 2D-CNNs find spatial features in images, 1D-CNNs
can slide across a time-series wave (like an ECG) to find localized
anomalies (like an inverted T-wave).
The Edge-to-Cloud Architecture (Healthcare IoT)
• Tier 1: The Edge (Sensors): Microcontrollers collect vitals. Basic noise
filtering (Low-pass/High-pass filters) is done here to save bandwidth.
• Tier 2: The Gateway (Smartphone): The wearable sends data via Bluetooth
to a phone, which acts as the MQTT or Kafka producer, securely streaming
it to the cloud.
• Tier 3: Stream Processing (Spark/Flink): Ingests the data, aligns
timestamps across different sensors (e.g., syncing the ECG stream with the
SpO2 stream), and handles missing packets.
• Tier 4: The AI Inference Engine: The LSTM model scores the live stream,
outputting a "Risk Probability Score" updated every second.
Addressing Alarm Fatigue (The False Positive)
• The Clinical Danger: If a monitor beeps 100 times a day for no reason,
nurses will eventually mute it or ignore it, potentially missing the 101st
beep, which is a real cardiac event.
• Multivariate Anomaly Detection: Instead of triggering an alarm because
one variable crossed a threshold, the AI looks at the correlation between
multiple variables.
• Contextual AI: The model uses accelerometer data to determine context. If
heart rate spikes but the accelerometer shows the patient is running, the
AI suppresses the alarm. If heart rate spikes and the accelerometer shows
the patient is lying completely still, the AI escalates the alarm to a doctor.
Case Study: Sepsis Prediction
• The Disease: Sepsis is a life-threatening response to infection. Every
hour of delayed treatment increases mortality by 8%.
• The Data: A multivariate time-series of heart rate, blood pressure,
temperature, and white blood cell count.
• The AI Triumph: Deep Learning models running on ICU data streams
can now predict the onset of sepsis up to 12 hours before clinical
recognition by human doctors, allowing for early antibiotic
intervention.
The Scale of Financial Data
• Volume: Global networks process upwards of 5,000 to 20,000
transactions per second globally (e.g., VisaNet).
• Variety: Transactions come from physical POS (Point of Sale), online
gateways, ATMs, and mobile apps.
• Veracity: Fraudsters actively try to mimic legitimate behavior. The
data is intentionally deceitful.
• Fraud represents less than 0.1% of all transactions. This is a massive
class-imbalance problem.
Real-Time Fraud Architecture
• Ingestion (Kafka): High-throughput streams capture the raw JSON
transaction payload from the merchant gateway.
• In-Memory NoSQL (Redis/Cassandra): The system instantly fetches
the user's "Profile" (average spend, frequent locations) in under 5ms.
A relational DB is too slow here.
• Stream Processing (Spark Streaming): Enriches the incoming Kafka
stream with the NoSQL profile data.
• AI Scoring (MLlib/XGBoost): Evaluates the enriched data, generating
a "Fraud Probability Score" (e.g., 0.92) in under 15ms
Supervised vs. Unsupervised ML for Fraud
• Supervised Learning (Classification): Algorithms: Random Forests,
Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Neural Networks.
• Pros: Highly accurate on known fraud patterns.
• Cons: Useless against "Zero-Day" fraud (new attack vectors the model has
never seen).
• Unsupervised Learning (Anomaly Detection):Algorithms: Isolation
Forests, K-Means Clustering, Autoencoders.
• Pros: Detects completely new, unseen behaviors (e.g., a sudden spike in
crypto purchases from a conservative account).
• Cons: Higher False Positive rate.
Graph Analytics for Anti-Money Laundering (AML)
• The AML Problem: Fraud isn't always a stolen card. It’s often
organized crime moving money through complex webs of accounts
("Smurfing").
• Why SQL Fails: Finding circular money movement in SQL requires
recursive JOINs, which are computationally devastating and take
hours to run.
• Graph Databases (e.g., Neo4j): Store data as Nodes (Accounts) and
Edges (Transactions).
• Graph Machine Learning: Algorithms like PageRank or Connected
Components can instantly detect a "Ring" of 50 accounts passing the
same $10,000 to each other.
Evaluation – The Cost of a False Positive
• Accuracy is a Lie: If a dataset has 99.9% legit transactions and 0.1%
fraud, a model that simply predicts "Legit" every single time is 99.9%
accurate. It is also 100% useless.
• Precision vs. Recall Trade-off: High Recall: Catch every fraudster, but
decline a lot of innocent customers (High False Positives).
• High Precision: Only block transactions you are 100% sure are fraud, but let
some bad guys slip through (High False Negatives).
• The Business Metric: Financial institutions tune models based on
"Customer Friction" tolerance vs. "Chargeback Risk."
Retail and E-commerce Analytics
• E-commerce changes the laws of physics.
• When you open Amazon, Flipkart, or Netflix, you are not visiting a
static store. The 'store' you see was computationally assembled
specifically for you in the last 100 milliseconds.
• If 100 million people log in at the same time, the server renders 100

Clickstream Analysis
• The "Invisible" Data: A user might buy 2 items a month, but they
generate thousands of data points daily (hovering over an image,
pausing on a review, adding to a cart and abandoning it). This is the
clickstream.
• The Big Data Pipeline: Relational databases cannot handle millions of
concurrent clickstream events.
• The Architecture:
• Web browser triggers JavaScript events.
• Events are sent to Apache Kafka (Ingestion).
• Spark Streaming processes the data (calculating session length, bounce rates).
• Data is stored in a NoSQL database (e.g., Cassandra) for real-time
dashboarding.
Recommendation Systems
• Content-Based Filtering: Recommends items similar to those the user
liked in the past, based on item metadata (e.g., "If you bought a sci-fi
book, here is another sci-fi book").
• Collaborative Filtering: The powerhouse of Big Data. It recommends
items based on the behavior of similar users. (e.g., "Users who
bought what you bought also bought this").
• Hybrid Systems: The industry standard. Combines both approaches to
solve the "Cold Start Problem" (what to recommend to a brand new
user with no history).
Matrix Factorization & Spark ALS
• The User-Item Matrix: Imagine a massive spreadsheet where rows
are millions of users, columns are millions of products, and the cells
contain ratings. It is incredibly sparse (most cells are empty because
you haven't bought most things).
• Matrix Factorization: The math of breaking down this massive matrix
into two smaller, dense matrices (User Features and Item Features) to
predict the missing values.
• Alternating Least Squares (ALS): The algorithm natively built into
Spark MLlib. It is highly parallelizable, allowing a cluster to calculate
recommendations for millions of users simultaneously.
Market Basket Analysis
• The Goal: Finding relationships between items. The classic example:
“People who buy diapers often buy beer on Fridays.”
• The Metrics:
• Support: How often do these items appear together in all transactions?
• Confidence: If they buy item A, how likely are they to buy item B?
• Lift: Does buying item A increase the likelihood of buying item B compared to
random chance?
• The Algorithms: Apriori (good for small data) and FP-Growth
(Frequent Pattern Growth - optimized for Big Data and supported in
Spark MLlib because it avoids scanning the database multiple times).
Customer Segmentation & Churn Prediction
• RFM Analysis: Segmenting customers based on Recency (how
recently they bought), Frequency (how often), and Monetary Value
(how much they spent).
• K-Means Clustering: Taking the RFM data and using unsupervised
learning to group customers into "Whales" (high spenders),
"Loyalists," and "At-Risk" segments.
• Churn Prediction: Using Classification algorithms (Logistic Regression,
Random Forest) to predict the probability that a customer will stop
using the service in the next 30 days, allowing the marketing team to
send a proactive discount code.
Smart City – A Massive Computer
• The CCTV cameras, smart meters, and induction loops in the asphalt
are the sensory inputs—the APIs. The 5G and fiber optic networks are
the system bus.
• But right now, this massive computer is running blindly.
• The traffic lights operate on dumb, static timers.
• The power grid generates electricity without knowing exactly who needs it,
leading to massive waste.
• If a pipe bursts underground, we only know about it because water floods the
street.
• That is Reactive Maintenance on a municipal scale.
The Scale of Urban IoT Data
• Volume & Velocity: A city of 1 million people might have 500,000
smart meters sending power readings every 15 minutes, plus
thousands of traffic cameras streaming 1080p video at 30 FPS.
• Variety: The data is a messy mix of unstructured video (CCTV),
structured time-series data (smart meters), and geospatial
coordinates (GPS from public transit).
• The Bandwidth Bottleneck: It is physically and financially impossible
to stream 10,000 HD video feeds to a centralized AWS or Google
Cloud server for real-time processing. The network would collapse.
Edge, Fog, and Cloud Computing Architecture
• Edge Computing: Pushing the AI directly onto the sensor. For
example, a smart camera running a lightweight model (like YOLO-tiny)
that only sends a text payload: {"intersection": 4, "car_count": 12}
instead of sending the whole video.
• Fog Computing: Intermediate processing nodes (like a local
neighborhood server) that aggregate data from multiple Edge devices
to make localized decisions (e.g., syncing a corridor of 5 traffic lights).
• Cloud Computing: The centralized data lake where historical data is
stored to train massive, city-wide predictive models over months or
years.
Smart Traffic Management (Computer Vision)
• The Problem with Timers: Static traffic lights cause "Phantom Traffic
Jams," where cars wait at a red light while the cross-street is
completely empty.
• Object Detection Models: Using CNNs like YOLO (You Only Look
Once) to accurately detect and count cars, buses, bicycles, and
pedestrians in real-time.
• Reinforcement Learning (RL): Training an AI agent using Q-Learning.
The agent is rewarded for maximizing the "throughput" of cars and
minimizing wait times, allowing the traffic grid to dynamically adapt
to rush hour or accidents.
Smart Grids and Predictive Energy Consumption
• The Energy Matching Problem: Electricity is difficult to store at a city
scale. Power plants must generate exactly what the city consumes at
any given millisecond.
• Time-Series Forecasting: Using LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory)
networks or ARIMA models to predict power demand based on
historical usage, weather forecasts, and special events.
• Anomaly Detection: Identifying sudden, unexplained drops in
pressure in smart water pipes or voltage anomalies in power
substations, alerting repair crews before a total blackout or flood
occurs.
Privacy, Security, and Federated Learning
• The Surveillance Risk: A city covered in smart cameras and
microphones is a massive privacy liability.
• Data Anonymization: ML pipelines must automatically blur faces and
license plates at the Edge before any video leaves the local device.
• Federated Learning: Training ML models across multiple
decentralized edge devices without ever exchanging the raw, sensitive
user data. The local devices only share the "model weight updates"
with the central server.
Static vs. Streaming Evaluation
• Static Evaluation (Hold-out Test Set): Data is shuffled and split. The
assumption is that the training data and the test data are drawn from
the exact same statistical distribution (i.i.d. - Independent and
Identically Distributed).
• Streaming Evaluation: Data arrives in a continuous, infinite sequence.
The statistical distribution of the data at time t might be entirely
different from time t-1000.
• The "Ground Truth" Latency: In a static test, you immediately know if
the model was right or wrong. In streaming (e.g., predicting if a
customer will churn in 30 days), you have to wait 30 days to get the
label to evaluate the prediction you made today.
The Enemy of Streaming AI – ‘Drift’
• Covariate Shift (Data Drift): The distribution of the input features (X)
changes, but the relationship to the target (Y) remains the same.
(e.g., A temperature sensor starts degrading and reads 2°C hotter, but
the machine is actually fine).
• Prior Probability Shift (Label Drift): The distribution of the target
variable (Y) changes. (e.g., A sudden spike in actual credit card fraud
during a holiday weekend).
• Concept Drift: The fundamental relationship between X and Y
changes. (e.g., The 2020 pandemic changing what a "normal"
transaction looks like).

Time-Decaying Metrics (Fading Factors)
• The Problem with Cumulative Accuracy: If a model has been running
for a year and processed 10 million events, a sudden drop in accuracy
today will barely affect the "Total Lifetime Accuracy." The metric hides
the current failure.
• Fading Factor (α): We apply an exponential moving average to our
evaluation metrics (like Accuracy or F1-Score), where 0 < α < 1.
• Formula: Metric_current = α · (Current_Error) + (1 - α) ·
Metric_previous
• Result: The evaluation metric "forgets" the distant past and is highly
sensitive to recent performance degradation.
