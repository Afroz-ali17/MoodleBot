Process Methodologies
Case Study: Voya Financial and Continuous Improvement
Voya Financial, a financial services firm, successfully executed an Initial Public Offering (IPO) in May 2013,
raising $1.27 billion. This IPO was not a fresh launch but rather a spin-off from its parent company, ING, the
largest bank in the Netherlands. The primary reason for this spin-off was to pay down obligations from the
government bailout that ING had received during the financial crisis of 2009.
However, beyond financial restructuring, the management of Voya saw this as a strategic opportunity to
transform the organization. They aimed to reimagine the company culture and streamline operations to create a
more efficient and customer-focused enterprise.
Challenges of Organizational Complexity
Voya was not a single, unified entity but rather a collection of multiple acquired companies. This meant that
the organization had a mixture of different corporate cultures. Such diversity of cultures made it difficult to
implement large-scale and comprehensive improvement initiatives.
Creation of the Continuous Improvement Center
To overcome these challenges, Voya established the Continuous Improvement Center (CIC), with the CEO
personally leading the initiative. The establishment of this center highlighted the importance of creating
centralization, transparency, and a holistic culture within the organization.
Continuous improvement is defined as a long-term strategy aimed at improving organizational processes
continuously. It is never a one-time project but rather an ongoing cycle. For continuous improvement to
succeed, several core factors are necessary:
• Transparency in operations
• Focus on measurable quality results and efficiency
• Employee training (a majority of Voya employees were trained in these methods)
• Tracking and monitoring progress, such as through employee engagement metrics, customer
satisfaction scores, and turnover rates
The ultimate goal of these efforts was to better serve customers, aligning organizational culture with
customer-centric outcomes.
Notable Outcomes of the Continuous Improvement Program
Voya’s continuous improvement program delivered significant, measurable benefits. Some of the key results
include:
• A 20% reduction in not-in-good-order (NIGO) applications within the Retirement Recordkeeping
Operations group since early 2017
• A 41% reduction in escalations on hardship calls to the call center
• A 54% reduction in internal quality control check failures
Additionally, the program fostered innovation. One major outcome was the creation of myOrangeMoney, an
online interactive website designed to help customers understand and manage their retirement savings. A
survey showed that this tool achieved an 80% customer satisfaction rate.
Link Between Continuous Improvement and RPA
Jeff Machols, Vice President of the Continuous Improvement Center at Voya Financial, highlighted the role of
continuous improvement in preparing the organization for Robotic Process Automation (RPA). According to
him, investing time in strengthening culture and continuous improvement was critical for adopting RPA.
Voya began its RPA journey in early 2018 by selecting and implementing UiPath, a leading RPA platform.
RPA was seen as a natural fit with Voya’s ongoing transformation efforts because it streamlined operations
without requiring heavy IT integration.
Technology and People: A Balanced Perspective
Machols emphasized that RPA and technology alone are not enough. For technology to generate real value,
employees must understand why the technology matters and how it delivers results. This reinforces the
principle that organizational culture, employee engagement, and technology adoption must go hand in hand.
Broader Context: Lean, Six Sigma, and Lean Six Sigma
The case of Voya demonstrates how continuous improvement connects with widely known process
methodologies such as Lean, Six Sigma, and Lean Six Sigma. These methodologies aim to improve
efficiency, reduce waste, ensure quality, and drive long-term innovation. Such approaches are particularly
relevant in the context of RPA, where process optimization must precede automation for sustainable results.

Lean
Early Origins of Lean: Henry Ford and the Assembly Line
In 1913, Henry Ford revolutionized capitalism and industrial production by introducing the moving assembly
line. This innovation allowed vehicles to be manufactured within hours rather than days or months. As a result,
Ford Motor Company quickly became one of the most valuable companies in the world. However, this system
had significant drawbacks. It was rigid, monolithic, and treated workers as mere cogs in a vast machine. These
issues became major weaknesses during the 1970s and 1980s, when foreign competition, particularly from
Japan, intensified and exposed the inefficiencies of the U.S. auto industry.
Toyota and the Birth of the Toyota Production System
The catalyst for change came from Toyota. In 1950, Eiji Toyoda, a member of the founding family, visited a
Ford plant. While impressed by the scale, he recognized that mass production was unsuitable for Japan, a
smaller country with more diverse customer needs. This insight led to the creation of the Toyota Production
System (TPS), which uniquely combined low costs, high quality, and mass customization.
Initially, Toyota’s innovations received little global recognition, as the company was seen as a marginal player
producing substandard cars. However, through continuous improvement (kaizen), Toyota consistently
produced better-quality, low-cost vehicles. Ironically, U.S. auto companies later scrambled to learn from
Toyota, seeking guidance on building competitive cars.
Influences: Total Quality Management (TQM) and Deming
Toyota’s approach was not purely original but rather an amalgamation of existing process methodologies,
including Total Quality Management (TQM). TQM was heavily influenced by W. Edwards Deming, who
emphasized the use of statistics to improve quality. While Deming struggled to gain traction in the U.S., his
methods found enthusiastic adoption in Japan, shaping Toyota’s philosophy of quality-driven manufacturing.
Coining of the Term Lean
The term “lean” itself was coined in the 1980s by Jim Womack, an MIT Ph.D. and consultant, to describe
Toyota’s methods. Lean was framed as a structured process methodology focusing on eliminating waste,
improving efficiency, and delivering customer value.
Core Principles of Lean
Lean’s success can be traced to its five core principles:
• Value: Defined as what the customer believes is important and worth paying for. Determining this
requires studying market trends, changes in customer tastes, and direct feedback.
• Value Stream: Once value is understood, the entire process from development to distribution is
mapped to identify and eliminate waste. Waste includes long wait times, quality issues, and excessive
transportation costs, though some “necessary waste” may remain, such as meeting regulatory
requirements.
• Flow: Ensures smooth and efficient creation and delivery of products by breaking processes into small
steps and optimizing them across the entire organization, not just isolated departments.
• Pull: Inventory is seen as one of the biggest wastes. Lean promotes just-in-time production, where
products are produced only as needed, requiring strong alignment with customer demand.
• Perfection: Continuous improvement, or kaizen, is the ultimate goal. Every employee must be
empowered to seek improvements daily, embedding a culture of never-ending enhancement.
Adopting Lean: Mindset and Practical Steps
Implementing lean requires a cultural transformation. It is not about quick fixes but about long-term
commitment. A good starting point is to focus on solving one problem at a time, gaining experience, and
gradually tackling larger challenges. Communication and collaboration tools such as Slack, Asana, and
SmartSheet can play a vital role in enabling clear objectives, tracking progress, and keeping employees
aligned. Dave King, CMO at Asana, emphasized that without clarity of goals, employees often lose sight of the
company’s main objectives. With lean-enabled collaboration tools, Asana was able to efficiently manage
hundreds of community events each year, each requiring 115 tasks, using templates to track and automate
processes.
Variations of Lean: Customization by Companies
Lean is inherently flexible and often adapted to fit the unique needs of individual organizations. Many
companies develop their own customized versions of lean methodologies.
Case Example: Danaher and the Danaher Business System (DBS)
A famous example of lean customization is Danaher Corporation. Originally founded in 1969 as a real estate
investment trust, it transitioned into industrial manufacturing through acquisitions. Its first major challenge
came with the merger of Jacobs Manufacturing and Chicago Pneumatic, forming a company producing brakes
and industrial equipment. At the time, the company was under severe pressure and nearing bankruptcy.
To rescue the company, the Rales brothers, founders of Danaher, enlisted Toyota production experts to
analyze their operations. The feedback was blunt—the factory was in disarray. However, with lean-based
restructuring, significant improvements were possible. One notable change was shifting from clockwise to
counterclockwise assembly, recognizing that the average person’s right arm is 3% stronger, leading to
measurable productivity gains. This, along with numerous other lean interventions, transformed the company.
From these experiences, Danaher developed its own methodology, known as the Danaher Business System
(DBS). DBS was built on the philosophy that:
• Exceptional People → Outstanding Plans → World-Class Tools → Sustainable Processes →
Superior Performance.
This cycle then attracts even more exceptional people, creating a self-sustaining loop of improvement.
The company’s priorities were summarized as Quality, Delivery, Cost, and Innovation.
Kaizen and Leadership Commitment at Danaher
To embed lean deeply, Danaher implemented kaizen sessions and policy deployment reviews across
divisions. Executives, including the CEO, personally taught lean courses for two weeks each year. Former CEO
Larry Culp not only led these efforts but also wrote daily commentary on kaizen initiatives and hosted annual
conferences to highlight best practices.
Impact of Lean at Danaher
The results were extraordinary. From its first acquisition in 1986, Danaher’s market value grew from $400
million to $97 billion, not including the $23 billion value of Fortive, which was spun off in 2016. This success
positioned Danaher as one of the most remarkable lean-driven companies in modern history.

Six Sigma
Origins and Early Development
The roots of Six Sigma trace back nearly 100 years, with the use of statistical methods for manufacturing
quality control. However, the methodology gained structure and global recognition in the mid-1980s when
Motorola formalized the approach. Motorola’s first major success story was in its pocket pager business,
where Six Sigma significantly reduced defects and boosted performance. From there, Six Sigma gained traction
across industries.
One of the most influential advocates was Jack Welch, CEO of General Electric (GE). Under his leadership,
Six Sigma became deeply embedded within GE’s operations. Welch famously described Six Sigma as: “a
quality program that, when all is said and done, improves your customer’s experience, lowers your costs, and
builds better leaders.” This statement captured the essence of Six Sigma: not only as a statistical quality tool but
also as a management philosophy driving customer satisfaction, cost efficiency, and leadership development.
Core Idea of Six Sigma
At its foundation, Six Sigma is about disciplined and structured methods to improve quality by:
• Detecting defects in processes
• Identifying root causes of these defects
• Improving processes so that results are repeatable and sustainable
The backbone of Six Sigma is data-driven decision-making, ensuring that improvements are
measurable and not based on assumptions.
Statistical Meaning of Six Sigma
The term Six Sigma comes directly from statistics, where “sigma” refers to a standard deviation—a measure
of variability in a dataset.
• A normal curve (bell curve) represents the probability distribution of a population. The mean is at the
center, and standard deviations measure how spread out the data points are.
• In real-world phenomena such as human height or weight, most values fall within one or two standard
deviations. For example, 68.26% of the data falls within the first standard deviation.
When applied to processes:
• A process operating at Six Sigma quality produces only 3.4 defects per million opportunities
(DPMO), which is near perfection.
• Most processes, however, operate between 3 sigma and 4 sigma, producing significantly higher defect
rates. Six Sigma techniques aim to push processes closer to the six-sigma benchmark by
systematically reducing variability and defects.
Measuring Defects: DPO and DPMO
To monitor and compare processes, Six Sigma uses metrics such as:
• Defects per Opportunity (DPO):
Formula:
DPO=Total Number of Defects ÷ Total Number of Defect Opportunities
• Defects per Million Opportunities (DPMO):
This scales DPO to a per-million basis for easy comparison.
Example Calculation:
Suppose a car company has two processes:
1. Mirror Attachment – 20 steps involved
2. Engine Assembly – 1,000 steps involved
In one production run, both processes show 5,000 defects. At first glance, it seems both performed equally
poorly. But with Six Sigma methods, we adjust for complexity:
• Mirrors:
DPO = 5,000 ÷ (20 × 5,000) = 0.05
DPMO = 50,000 defects per million opportunities
• Engines:
DPO = 5,000 ÷ (1,000 × 5,000) = 0.001
DPMO = 1,000 defects per million opportunities
Although both had 5,000 defects, the engine process is far superior because of its complexity.

Applications Beyond Manufacturing
While Six Sigma’s origins lie in manufacturing, the methodology has proven adaptable to a wide range of
industries, including services, healthcare, finance, and IT. The core reason is universal: all organizations have
processes that can be improved to reduce waste, improve quality, enhance efficiency, and ultimately deliver
better value to customers.

How to Implement Six Sigma
Overview of Six Sigma Implementation
There are multiple ways to apply Six Sigma principles to organizational processes. However, the most widely
used and structured approach is the DMAIC methodology, an acronym for Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve,
and Control (commonly pronounced as duh-may-ick). This framework provides a systematic way to identify
problems, analyze data, implement improvements, and ensure long-term sustainability.
Define
The first step involves assembling a team and assigning a project name to create ownership and clarity. The
team must then identify a specific problem to solve, such as reducing delivery time, improving customer
satisfaction scores, or eliminating recurring errors. A proper timeline with clear milestones must be established
to track progress. To formalize this process, teams typically create a project charter, which acts as a written plan
outlining objectives, scope, responsibilities, and deliverables.
Measure
In this stage, the goal is to map out the existing process, often using flowcharts or process maps. This provides
a visual representation of the workflow and helps identify potential inefficiencies. Having multiple people
review the flowchart ensures accuracy and avoids blind spots. After mapping, the team develops a data
collection plan to determine what data is required, how it will be gathered, and from what sources. This step can
be time-consuming but is critical for accuracy, since poor data leads to faulty conclusions.
Analyze
The analysis phase focuses on identifying the root causes of the problem. This requires asking deeper questions
such as: What is really happening? Why is this problem occurring? To uncover these causes, teams may
conduct brainstorming sessions and use diagramming tools like the Fishbone Diagram (Ishikawa Diagram),
which helps categorize potential causes. Additionally, statistical methods such as regression analysis may be
used to determine correlations between variables and isolate the key drivers of defects or inefficiencies.
Improve
After identifying the root causes, the next step is to develop and implement solutions. However, caution is
necessary because solutions may bring unintended consequences or hidden risks. To mitigate these, teams
employ error-proofing techniques and quality control systems. A widely used tool here is Failure Modes and
Effects Analysis (FMEA), originally developed by the U.S. military during World War II and later applied in
the Apollo space program. FMEA systematically examines each process step, identifies potential failure points,
and prioritizes them based on their likelihood and severity, thereby reducing risk before implementation.
Control
Once solutions are implemented, the control phase ensures that improvements remain effective over time. This
involves setting up monitoring systems and conducting regular reviews to maintain process consistency.
Importantly, not all variations require corrective action, as processes naturally fluctuate due to random variance.
To distinguish between normal variation and genuine issues, teams use Statistical Process Control (SPC) charts.
These charts track performance over time using three reference lines: the average (centerline), the upper control
limit, and the lower control limit. If process variation exceeds these limits, corrective action is required.
Strategic Application of Six Sigma
Although Six Sigma is powerful, it is not practical to apply it to every process due to limitations in time,
resources, and cost. Instead, organizations should prioritize processes with the highest potential impact on
performance and customer satisfaction.
A central guiding principle here is the Voice of the Customer (VOC). This means listening to how customers
perceive the problem in their own terms. Once identified, these insights are translated into Critical-to-Quality
(CTQ) requirements, which must be important, measurable, and directly tied to customer expectations.
Examples include wait times, product quality, service accuracy, and on-time delivery.
Lean Thinking as a Complementary Philosophy
While Six Sigma emphasizes data-driven defect reduction, Lean methodology focuses on eliminating waste and
streamlining processes. As a broader philosophy, Lean has demonstrated effectiveness across diverse industries.
For example, a study by the American College of Emergency Physicians found that emergency rooms applying
Lean principles experienced measurable improvements in patient care quality, reduced wait times, and fewer
patients leaving without treatment. This highlights how both Six Sigma and Lean can complement each other in
driving operational excellence.

Six Sigma Roles and Levels of Proficiency
Executive
The executive plays a critical role in Six Sigma projects because their involvement demonstrates significant
buy-in from the top level of the organization. This ensures that the project gains traction and moves forward
effectively. The executive is responsible for setting the overall objectives of the project, defining the key goals,
and approving important actions or decisions. Without executive support, Six Sigma initiatives may struggle to
gain legitimacy and priority within the organization.
Champion
The champion is selected by the executive and is someone who has undergone Six Sigma training. This
individual takes on the responsibility of operational management of the project. Champions provide the
necessary resources to the team, ensure smooth execution, and actively work to remove roadblocks that may
hinder progress. Additionally, champions often need to coordinate across different departments to ensure that
processes align and that cross-functional collaboration is achieved. Their leadership helps maintain momentum
and ensures that the project objectives are met.
Process Owner
Each of the critical processes in a Six Sigma initiative requires a process owner. This individual helps manage
the team and ensures that the project remains on track. A process owner must have experience with statistical
techniques since data-driven decision-making is a central element of Six Sigma. Their responsibility is not only
to oversee the project’s progress but also to make sure improvements are sustainable in the long term within
their area of responsibility.
Levels of Proficiency in Six Sigma (Belt System)
White Belts
White belts represent novices in Six Sigma. They typically undergo only a few hours of basic training. Their
training covers the fundamentals of quality and process thinking, giving them an introductory awareness of Six
Sigma principles.
Yellow Belts
Yellow belts also represent a basic level of Six Sigma proficiency but with more understanding compared to
white belts. Their training covers areas such as the DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control)
methodology, variation in processes, and process mapping. A yellow belt may even hold roles such as
executive or champion in certain organizations because they have a basic understanding of Six Sigma tools and
methods.
Green Belts
Green belts work under the guidance of black belts and play a supporting role in Six Sigma projects. They are
responsible for helping implement Six Sigma systems within the organization. Their training involves learning
statistics and data analysis, which can take several months. Green belts often balance Six Sigma duties with
their regular work responsibilities.
Black Belts
Black belts are full-time Six Sigma specialists. Their role requires an in-depth understanding of advanced
statistical methods. They are responsible for leading projects, solving complex process problems, and driving
improvements within the organization. Black belts also mentor green belts and ensure proper application of Six
Sigma techniques.
Master Black Belts
This is the highest level of Six Sigma proficiency. Master black belts not only manage projects but also take
responsibility for training and mentoring others across the organization. They ensure consistent application of
Six Sigma methodologies and act as subject-matter experts. Their involvement ensures alignment between
organizational strategy and Six Sigma practices.
Certification and Training
It is important to note that there is no single official certification authority for Six Sigma. Many organizations
and training providers offer certification, and the available options are diverse, ranging from online to
classroom-based instruction. A simple online search reveals the wide variety of training paths available. Books
such as The Six Sigma Handbook and The Lean Six Sigma Pocket Toolbook are also excellent resources for
learning. However, while the Council for Six Sigma Certification sets standards for training, certification itself
is not universally standardized.
Key to Successful Implementation
Even though the belt structure is widely recognized, an organization does not necessarily need to have different
belts to achieve successful Six Sigma implementation. The true key to success lies in ensuring that the project
team understands and applies the fundamental principles of Six Sigma effectively. Commitment to continuous
improvement, focus on data-driven decision-making, and alignment with business goals are more important
than formal titles.
Supporting Software
Several software packages are available to assist in Six Sigma projects. One popular example is Minitab, which
provides powerful statistical tools. With Minitab, teams can create charts, conduct hypothesis testing, perform
regressions, and carry out various other statistical analyses. These tools enhance accuracy and efficiency in Six
Sigma projects by enabling teams to make informed, data-based decisions.

Lean Six Sigma
Introduction to Lean Six Sigma
Lean Six Sigma is a powerful process methodology that integrates the strengths of both lean and Six Sigma.
Lean emphasizes the elimination of waste, while Six Sigma focuses on statistics and data-driven techniques to
reduce variation and improve quality. The combination of these two approaches, known as Lean Six Sigma,
was introduced in 2001 with the publication of Leaning into Six Sigma: The Path to Integration of Lean
Enterprise and Six Sigma by Barbara Wheat, Chuck Mills, and Mike Carnell. This methodology has since
become a widely adopted strategy for achieving operational excellence across industries.
How to Use Lean Six Sigma
A common framework for using Lean Six Sigma is to begin with lean practices before layering in Six Sigma
methods. Lean tools, such as the 5 S’s, provide immediate impact by improving workplace efficiency and
eliminating unnecessary waste. Once lean is established, Six Sigma techniques like kaizen, DMAIC, or error-
proofing systems can be introduced to address process variation and quality issues.
The 5 S’s of Lean
Sort (Seiri in Japanese)
This step emphasizes clarity by eliminating unnecessary items, processes, and clutter. Removing what is not
needed creates efficiency and focus.
Straighten (Seiton)
This principle stresses organization by arranging resources and tools so that the right item is available at the
right time. For example, designing an office or factory floor for maximum efficiency ensures smoother
workflows.
Shine (Seiso)
This step ensures everything remains clean and tidy. It is not a one-time effort but must be a daily activity.
Additionally, there must be a focus on identifying and addressing the root causes of dirt or disorganization.
Standardize (Seiketsu)
This stage involves creating consistent, step-by-step processes for maintaining cleanliness and organization. It
requires establishing clear roles and responsibilities so that standards are consistently upheld.
Sustain (Shitsuke)
This is often the most challenging step because it requires maintaining discipline over time. It is about ensuring
that standards remain in place and that organizational commitment does not fade.
The Seven Types of Waste in Lean
Developed by Taiichi Ohno, the creator of the Toyota Production System, lean highlights seven categories of
waste that organizations should eliminate:
Motion
Poorly organized workflows can result in wasted movement of people and machines, leading to delays and
higher costs. Detailed workflow analysis helps reduce this waste.
Transportation
Unnecessary transportation is often a major waste source. Innovations by companies such as Lyft, Uber, and
Amazon show how improved logistics can reduce costs and improve efficiency.
Defects
Defects can stem from design flaws, manufacturing errors, or even harmful product elements, such as asbestos
in buildings. Regardless of type, defects must be minimized because even small errors can have costly
consequences. Strong quality control processes are essential.
Overproduction
Producing more than what is needed can be highly damaging, especially in industries like toys or apparel,
where consumer preferences change rapidly. Overproduction can be managed with demand forecasting tools,
analytics, and just-in-time manufacturing.
Inventory
Excess inventory, whether raw materials, work-in-progress, or finished goods, increases costs and risks
obsolescence. Dell Computer’s build-to-order strategy in the 1990s is a prime example of how managing
inventory effectively can provide a competitive advantage by reducing waste and improving cash flow.
Waiting
Waiting for suppliers, partners, or customers adds up to significant wasted time. Modern expectations,
influenced by companies like Amazon, require rapid and seamless service delivery.
Overprocessing
This waste occurs when unnecessary steps are added to processes, such as over-designing a product in ways
customers do not value. For instance, adding unnecessary complexity to an engine design that provides no
noticeable benefit to most customers.
Integration with Six Sigma
After establishing lean practices and achieving visible improvements, organizations can incorporate Six Sigma
tools for deeper quality and process control. Methods like kaizen and DMAIC provide structured approaches
for solving problems, analyzing root causes, and sustaining improvements.
Additional Tools in Lean Six Sigma
Beyond the 5 S’s and the seven wastes, Lean Six Sigma also employs other useful methodologies. One such
tool is poka yoke, which is derived from Japanese words meaning “avoid mistakes.” The purpose of poka yoke
is to design processes that minimize the chance of human error. This could be as simple as a checklist for
employees or as sophisticated as an automated system using robotic process automation (RPA). Such tools help
ensure accuracy, efficiency, and reliability within workflows.

Finding the Right Balance
Dangers of Overusing Process Methodologies
While lean, Six Sigma, and lean Six Sigma offer strong benefits, taking them to extremes can have harmful
consequences. This often happens when management’s primary focus is on aggressive cost-cutting. Although
such moves may deliver short-term gains, they can weaken a company’s ability to compete in the long term.
The Kraft Heinz Case Study
A notable example of imbalance is the merger of Kraft and Heinz in March 2015, valued at $49 billion, backed
by 3G Capital Partners and Warren Buffett. At the time, Buffett praised the deal, noting his excitement about
uniting two world-class organizations and delivering shareholder value. Initially, management emphasized cost
reductions and streamlined operations, and these actions seemed to work. However, cuts went too deep. Kraft
Heinz significantly reduced investments in R&D, marketing, and product development, which squeezed the
business at a time when consumer preferences were shifting toward healthier, lower-priced alternatives. The
result was severe: by February 2019, the company disclosed a $15.4 billion write-down of its Kraft and Oscar
Mayer brands and slashed its dividend. The stock price plunged from $80 to $32. Business management expert
John Kotter noted in the Harvard Business Review that Kraft Heinz’s situation highlights how traditional
restructuring methods are increasingly risky in a world of accelerating change. Any approach that hinders
innovation can lead to disastrous outcomes.
Applying Lean and Six Sigma to RPA
Commitment Required
Using lean, Six Sigma, or lean Six Sigma requires major investments of time and resources, which may even
delay a robotic process automation (RPA) implementation by six months to a year. Yet, this delay is often
worthwhile because redesigning processes first can maximize the impact of automation. Many leading
consulting and IT firms employ Six Sigma black belts who specialize in such redesign efforts, though some
organizations succeed with a do-it-yourself approach as well.
Need for Process Evaluation
Before automating, it is critical to examine existing processes and standard operating procedures, many of
which may have been created in an ad hoc manner with limited planning. This examination offers rich
opportunities to identify improvements before layering on RPA.
Using Process Maps
Creating process maps with tools such as Microsoft Visio or IBM Blueworks Live helps visualize workflows
and uncover redesign opportunities. These maps can also be shared with team members for collaborative
feedback. When creating process maps, several considerations must be kept in mind:
• Bottlenecks and Root Causes: Identify where the process slows down and what causes those
inefficiencies, then determine how to improve them.
• FMEA Thinking: Apply Failure Mode and Effects Analysis to anticipate unintended consequences
before implementing changes. Some functions may prove poor candidates for automation after this
review.
• Motion Analysis: Go beyond software by observing physical workflows in the office. Walk around to
see how employees perform their tasks at desks, which can reveal additional inefficiencies.
Implications of Automation
Automation has significant implications for error reduction. Humans are prone to mistakes in repetitive tasks,
so companies often insert checkpoints such as managerial reviews. With RPA, these steps may not be necessary
because bots execute tasks consistently every time—provided there has been rigorous testing, validation,
control, and audit before deployment.
Authority and Decision-Making
Traditional processes often require approvals, such as a manager authorizing a payment above a certain amount.
RPA can streamline this by embedding exception rules into the system. Furthermore, with advances in AI, bots
may eventually be capable of making certain decisions autonomously.
Continuous Improvement and Customer Focus
Once process maps are created and RPA is implemented, the effort should not stop there. In line with lean and
Six Sigma principles, continuous improvement must remain a core focus. Process maps should serve as the
foundation for ongoing refinement. Importantly, all changes should be evaluated against one central question:
how will this improve value for the customer?

Planning
Author and Context
The section is written by Tom Taulli, based in Monrovia, CA, USA. It emphasizes the importance of building
a solid foundation for RPA (Robotic Process Automation) implementation.
EY’s Role in RPA
Ernst & Young (EY) is highlighted as a strong believer in the potential of RPA. The firm not only maintains a
thriving RPA consulting practice but also leverages RPA internally to enhance its own global operations.
This establishes EY as both a practitioner and a consultant in the RPA landscape.
Criticisms and Challenges with RPA
Despite its success with RPA, EY is not blind to the pitfalls of implementation. The firm recognizes that like
any major enterprise software system, RPA is susceptible to potential risks and land mines. EY’s
consultants have the expertise to guide clients through these challenges. Interestingly, one of EY’s fastest-
growing business areas is providing remediation services for failed RPA implementations, reflecting the
frequency of such failures in the industry.
Report Findings on RPA Failures
In a comprehensive report covering RPA implementations across 20 countries, EY found discouraging
results. According to their analysis, 30% to 50% of initial RPA projects failed. This statistic underscores the
high failure rate of early-stage implementations.
Interpretation of Failures
EY is clear that these failures are not an indictment of the technology itself. In fact, the firm acknowledges
that RPA vendors generally provide solid software. Instead, the failures primarily stem from insufficient
planning and preparation on the part of organizations adopting RPA. This highlights that technology alone
cannot guarantee success—it requires a structured strategy and planning framework.
Importance of Planning in RPA
The key message emphasized is that considerable planning is essential for RPA success. Unfortunately, in
many organizations, this planning phase often receives scant attention, leading to project failures. The need to
prioritize planning is positioned as a critical step in achieving sustainable outcomes with RPA.
Focus of the Chapter
This chapter aims to outline the initial steps in the RPA journey. The objective is to help organizations create
a durable foundation that supports long-term success in their automation initiatives. By addressing planning
from the outset, companies can significantly reduce risks and maximize the benefits of RPA adoption.

The Preliminaries of RPA Implementation
Ease of Use and the Temptation to Rush
Robotic Process Automation (RPA) is often seen as relatively easy to use due to its interactive workflows and
drag-and-drop capabilities. However, this simplicity can create a problem: organizations may be tempted to
rush into implementation, creating multiple bots quickly to automate processes without proper planning. While
such an approach may seem agile and innovative, it can ultimately result in chaos and failure if not managed
carefully.
Importance of a Written Plan of Action
Instead of rushing, the better approach is to draft a clear written plan of action. This plan should outline key
priorities, objectives, and roles for the RPA implementation. It should also consider important aspects such as
security, the impact on IT systems, governance requirements, and compliance with regulations. By having a
well-defined strategy, organizations reduce the risk of disorganized or ineffective automation initiatives.
Brainstorming as the First Step
A good exercise to begin the process is brainstorming. At this stage, freewheeling ideas should be encouraged
without criticism. The aim is to unleash creativity and ingenuity. Feedback should be collected from different
departments across the organization, including marketing, finance, HR, legal, IT, and sales.
Guiding Questions During Discovery
During this discovery mode, some important questions to ask include:
• What is the current degree of automation in your department?
• What processes are already working well, and why?
• Which processes are falling short?
• Which processes could be improved with automation?
• Which processes are repetitive and routine, and how many people are involved in them?
• How much time is currently being spent on these processes?
• What technologies have failed in the past, and why?
• What is the general employee sentiment toward automation? Is there resistance or fear of job loss?
Tools for Tracking Ideas
It is important to track ideas and enable commenting during brainstorming. Even simple tools like a Google
document can be highly effective. This ensures collaboration, transparency, and clarity in capturing insights.
Workshops for Ideation and Training
Many companies set up workshops to intensively focus on ideation for a few days. These workshops also
provide an opportunity to offer training, including tutorials on the fundamentals of RPA, its benefits, and how it
will impact daily work.
Real-World Example: CloudStorm Workshop
CloudStorm, a company that provides RPA services, conducts one-day workshops for its clients. The structure
of such workshops typically includes:
• Introduction to Software Bots and Automation: A general introduction to RPA, including how it
works, relevant use cases for the client’s industry, the types of tasks most suitable for automation, and
potential pitfalls to avoid.
• Process Automation Identification and Elaboration: Interactive group activities designed to generate
automation ideas. These are often pinned on a whiteboard or wall, followed by discussions where the
team evaluates and scores the ideas based on feasibility, cost, and potential return, often using voting
methods.
• Discussion and Validation: Teams are divided to present and validate the proposed automation ideas
through structured discussions.
Next Steps After the Workshop
Following the workshop, CloudStorm drafts a detailed report summarizing the results. The report suggests areas
suitable for automation, estimates the expected impact, and defines the Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) that
will be used to measure success.
Process Mining for Mapping and Optimization
Some organizations go further by using specialized software to support process mapping and optimization. This
practice is known as process mining. It helps in identifying inefficiencies and opportunities for automation,
ensuring a data-driven and optimized RPA implementation strategy.

Using a Consulting Firm for RPA Implementation
Common Practice and Initial Concerns
It is quite common for companies to use consulting firms when planning an RPA implementation. However,
this decision comes with challenges. Consulting services can be costly, and it may be difficult to find a firm that
is both qualified and suited to the company’s specific needs. In some cases, the consulting firm may lack direct
experience with the company’s industry or business size, making them less effective as partners.
Advantages That Justify the Cost
Despite the challenges, hiring a consulting firm can bring significant advantages that often outweigh the costs—
provided the right partner is chosen. Importantly, consulting engagements do not always need to be long term.
For example, in the case of Symantec’s RPA implementation, the company retained a consulting firm only for
the first couple of months to help with initial planning and bot development. Afterward, the internal team took
over. This shows that firms can be leveraged flexibly to add expertise at crucial stages.
Benefits of Using a Consulting Firm
• Experience in Identifying Automation Opportunities: A good consulting firm has seen the full
lifecycle of multiple implementations and understands common pitfalls, danger points, and success
factors. This knowledge greatly increases the chances of success.
• Fresh Perspective on Processes: Internal teams may be too close to existing workflows and miss
opportunities for automation. Consultants bring an external view and technical knowledge that helps
uncover both obvious and hidden automation opportunities.
• Support in RPA Platform Selection: Consulting firms can be very helpful in evaluating and selecting
the most suitable RPA platforms and related software. However, caution is needed—some firms may
specialize in or have financial ties to specific vendors, which could bias their recommendations. It is
therefore advisable to choose firms that are vendor-agnostic.
• Proprietary Frameworks and Methodologies: Larger consulting firms often bring their own tested
frameworks and methodologies, which are standardized strategies proven across industries. These can
make RPA planning and execution easier to understand and adopt.
• In-House Tools and Software: Some firms develop proprietary tools, such as systems to evaluate
processes, prebuilt libraries, and ready-made bots, which can accelerate implementation.
• Next-Level Implementation with AI: A consulting firm can help organizations expand beyond basic
RPA into more advanced areas like artificial intelligence. Since AI adoption often requires significant
time and resources, consultants can smooth the process and achieve actionable results faster.
• Training and Education Resources: Many firms provide training for employees along with self-
learning materials, interactive tools, and videos. This helps build internal expertise while ensuring
smooth adoption.
• Assistance with Center of Excellence (CoE): A CoE is a team that manages the RPA program within
an organization. Consulting firms can help assemble this team and may even participate as members.
• Compliance and Governance Expertise: Reputable consulting firms have in-house capabilities to
manage compliance and regulatory complexities. They can build risk assessments, oversight systems,
and controls directly into the RPA design.
• Result-Based Business Models: Some firms operate on performance-based contracts, where payments
are linked to achieving milestones. This aligns their goals with the client’s outcomes.
• Robotics-as-a-Service Option: Certain consulting firms offer RPA hosting on their own servers,
lowering costs and simplifying project management.
Risks and Selection Considerations
The rapid growth of the RPA industry has led to a surge in consulting firms. Since it is relatively easy to start
an RPA consulting practice, the market is crowded, which increases risks for clients. This makes it critical to
interview multiple firms, carefully check references, and ensure credibility.
Importance of Local or On-Site Presence
It is also essential that the consulting firm be local or at least willing to travel to the client’s site. Relying solely
on virtual arrangements is not advisable, as it can be difficult for consultants to properly evaluate company
processes without being physically present.

RPA Consulting: Some Case Studies
Case Study 1: Cognizant and a Major Insurance Company
For a large insurance company, Cognizant played a key role in driving an RPA initiative. The company had a
high volume of manual processes across various departments that could benefit from automation. The first
focus area was in Australia, particularly within the HR and finance departments. This was a significant
undertaking, as Cognizant had to assess the back-office processes involving about 2,000 employees. To ensure
efficiency, Cognizant held extensive discussions with department leaders, which helped to identify and narrow
down the potential automation targets. The initial findings revealed over 100 automation opportunities, with a
potential of $7 million in net savings. This would allow the redeployment of more than 80 employees to higher-
value tasks. To keep the project manageable, Cognizant further refined the list and performed an in-depth
analysis of the shortlisted processes. The outcome was a detailed report outlining the goals, deliverables, and
ROI expectations. While the insurance company could have carried out the project internally, it is likely that it
would have taken longer and involved more errors without Cognizant’s consulting expertise.
Case Study 2: Genpact and a Large Aerospace Firm
Genpact, another leading consulting firm, supported a large aerospace company in its RPA journey, integrating
artificial intelligence alongside automation. Genpact provided advisory services that included process
assessment, software selection, designing a Center of Excellence (CoE), and initiating a pilot program. The
implementation led to major productivity gains, with savings between 30% and 50%. Additionally, the
company projected an 8X return on investment over a three-year period, showcasing the significant long-term
financial benefits of the project.
Case Study 3: Deloitte and a Global Bank
A global bank was facing challenges in handling increasing volumes of complex transactions while dealing
with data scattered across silos. The bank wanted to achieve higher efficiency without increasing its workforce.
To address this, Deloitte was brought in to analyze and implement an RPA strategy. Within just six weeks,
Deloitte successfully created and deployed the first bot. Encouraged by this quick success, the bank scaled up
rapidly, introducing bots for credit card remediation, PDF conversion, and payment processing. Ultimately, the
bank deployed more than 150 bots that processed about 120,000 requests each week at only 30% of the cost of
human labor. Over three years, the estimated cost savings amounted to around $40 million, making this one of
the most impactful RPA consulting projects.
Case Study 4: EY and a Global Rental Car Company
A global rental car company wanted to transform its shared services center located in Budapest. The company
had relied heavily on Excel and basic scripting, which were proving insufficient for its expanding operations.
To solve this, EY was engaged to run an RPA pilot program. EY helped in identifying key areas for
automation, selecting the most appropriate tools, and building the necessary bots. The initial pilot showed
strong results, projecting savings equivalent to 10 to 20 full-time employees or about €1 million over three
years, translating into an impressive 900% ROI. The success of the pilot encouraged the company to consider
scaling up the RPA program to cover 200 to 400 full-time equivalents over two years, thus multiplying the
benefits even further.

What to Automate?
Need for Careful Selection
At first glance, selecting processes to automate may seem straightforward. However, effective RPA
implementation requires a proper analysis of existing workflows to gain a holistic view. This analysis helps in
identifying the best automation opportunities. It is equally important to recognize that RPA is not a universal
solution for all processes; it works best for specific types of tasks. Additionally, the evaluation team should
consist of people with diverse expertise, including technology, business operations, and day-to-day
departmental knowledge.
Key Factors for RPA Suitability
• Tedious Work: Tasks involving little knowledge, such as copy-paste or repeated clicking, are ideal
since they waste skilled employee time.
• Time-Consuming Processes: Work that delays outcomes, like report generation or end-of-quarter
processing.
• Repetitive Tasks: Processes with fixed, unchanging steps fit well with RPA’s mechanical approach.
• Frequency: Recurring activities performed multiple times a week yield higher value when automated.
• Rules-Based Processes: RPA excels in workflows with IF/Then logic, ensuring consistency.
• Clearly Defined Processes: Having metrics, step counts, and flow charts helps identify good
candidates.
• High-Volume Activities: Software handles repetitive, high-speed work better than humans.
• Error-Prone Areas: Where manual input often leads to mistakes.
• Lack of API Availability: If no API exists, RPA may substitute effectively.
• Customization Limitations: Instead of costly vendor customizations, RPA provides a cost-effective
option.
• Sensitive Data Handling: Bots can enhance protection and reduce human exposure risks.
• Scaling Limitations: When IT systems are rigid, RPA offers scalability without additional headcount.
• Organizational Bottlenecks: RPA helps in breaking down silos and easing process blockages.
Practical Evaluation Example
To decide what to automate, diagnostic scoring can be applied. For instance, in a customer cancellation process:
• Frequency per week: 7
• Potential time savings: 2 hours
• Steps in the process: 12
• Human intervention required: 2
Comparisons across processes help prioritize the best candidates.
Strategy for First Automations
The initial focus should not be purely on cost savings. Instead, select processes with fewer steps, limited cross-
departmental involvement, and low approval requirements. This enables quick wins, accelerates learning, and
motivates the team to pursue larger goals later.
Common Tasks Suitable for RPA
• Data Migration: Automating information transfers between multiple systems reduces wasted time and
errors.
• Data Updates: Automating customer detail changes across systems removes inefficiency.
• Tracking: Bots can monitor changes and detect issues proactively.
• Alerts: Bots can flag when human decision-making is required, seamlessly integrating humans into
workflows.
Importance of Documentation
Once processes are chosen, documentation is essential. It need not be overly detailed but should include:
• Rationales for automating
• Step-by-step flows or charts
• Names of workers involved
• IT assets connected
• Security, compliance, and governance requirements
Processes Unsuitable for RPA
According to Aaron Bultman, Director of Product for Nintex RPA, tasks involving creative thinking,
brainstorming, or physical actions (like retrieving paper files) are better handled by humans. However, hybrid
approaches are possible, where repetitive parts are automated while human tasks remain manual. Workflow
automation can hand off tasks between humans and bots, maximizing efficiency and ensuring only meaningful
work is left to humans.
Back-Office vs. Front-Office
It is often best to begin with back-office functions since they are rules-based and repetitive. Front-office
functions carry more variability, including unpredictable customer interactions. Poorly developed bots in these
areas risk creating negative customer experiences.

ROI for RPA
Challenge of Measuring ROI Early
In the initial stages of RPA implementation, measuring ROI can be difficult. However, it is still important to
establish some milestones and tracking mechanisms. A complete and accurate sense of ROI often requires
waiting at least a year.
Return Component of ROI
The primary “return” in the ROI equation comes from cost savings, especially through reduced dependency on
full-time equivalent employees (FTEs).
Example Calculation
Suppose the invoicing process requires four FTEs, each paid $20 per hour, working 40 hours weekly. The
annual labor cost is $166,400. With RPA, costs are reduced by 60%, lowering labor expenses to $58,240 per
year. This yields a net gain of $108,160 ($166,400 – $58,240). Considering that the annual RPA system cost is
$80,000, the ROI unfolds as follows:
• Year 1: $108,160 saved → ROI 35.2%
• Year 2: $216,320 saved → ROI 170%
• Year 3: $324,480 saved → ROI 306%
This demonstrates that the project becomes highly profitable over time.
Broader Measurement of Returns
Beyond financial savings, ROI should include other dimensions:
• Accuracy: Assess reduction in errors and improvements in data quality due to automation.
• Customer Satisfaction: Measure improvement using CSAT (Customer Satisfaction Score) or NPS (Net
Promoter Score, ranging –100 to 100).
• Agility: Track how much faster processes are executed, given that bots work 24/7 at high speed.
• Employee Satisfaction: Use surveys to check for morale improvements, reduced absenteeism, and
lower turnover.
• Innovation: Evaluate whether employees have more time for higher-value tasks, creativity, and
developing new initiatives.
• Analytics: Leverage improved process tracking to gain insights and identify new optimization
opportunities.
Qualitative Benefits
Some benefits, while not directly financial, still enhance ROI reporting: improved reliability, better compliance,
and stronger organizational governance. These can strengthen stakeholder buy-in for RPA adoption.
Cost Side of ROI
The main costs include license or subscription fees for RPA software. Other expenses may involve:
• Prebuilt bots
• Servers and hosting services
• Additional supporting software
• Labor costs for implementation, bot development, and monitoring
• Consulting firm engagement for specialized support
Because of these varying expenses, many organizations adopt the total cost of ownership (TCO) model, which
captures both direct costs (e.g., licenses) and indirect costs (e.g., training, infrastructure, ongoing support).
Economies of Scale and Diminishing Returns
Over time, economies of scale emerge—certain expenses like training decline. However, returns may also
diminish, as the easiest automation opportunities (“low-hanging fruit”) are exhausted, leaving fewer impactful
processes to automate.

The Plan for RPA Implementation
High-Level Objectives and Goals
When creating an RPA implementation plan, the first step is to clearly set out the high-level objectives and
goals. These goals act as the guiding roadmap for the project. The most common suggestions include reducing
the Full-Time Equivalent (FTE) time spent on processes, improving cycle times and turnaround times, and
lowering the number of errors in routine operations. Although such estimations may not always be perfectly
accurate, it is important to establish them as clear-cut milestones. These milestones help in setting direction,
and if they turn out to be either too challenging or not ambitious enough, adjustments can always be made
midway through the project.
Alignment with Digital Transformation Goals
The RPA plan should align with the company’s overall digital transformation strategy. This ensures that the
automation efforts are not isolated but instead contribute meaningfully to the larger organizational vision.
Furthermore, to highlight the significance of the project and secure commitment, the plan should have a
champion or sponsor, preferably from the executive leadership. Having such a sponsor signals organizational
importance and guarantees stronger backing for the RPA initiative.
Core Team Formation and Leadership
The plan must outline the structure and composition of the core team. Typically, this team is made up of
individuals from diverse departments, ensuring that different perspectives and expertise are brought together.
Since many team members may not be dedicated full-time to the RPA initiative, the presence of a strong leader
becomes crucial. Leadership provides direction, maintains focus, and ensures accountability within the team.
Clear Roles and Responsibilities
It is essential for the plan to clearly define the roles and tasks of each team member. This clarity eliminates
confusion, improves coordination, and ensures smooth progress. Later, the concept of a Center of Excellence
(CoE) can also be introduced as a structured approach to sustaining and scaling RPA initiatives, but for the
initial plan, role definition is key.
Involvement of the IT Department
The IT department must be actively involved in the planning process. Their role is vital to address concerns
related to system integration, data security, and governance. Without IT’s support, the project could face major
roadblocks in terms of compatibility and compliance.
RPA Vendor Evaluation Factors
Another critical aspect of the plan is the evaluation of potential RPA vendors. This includes assessing the
strengths, capabilities, and suitability of vendors in terms of the organization’s unique requirements. Although
detailed vendor evaluation will be studied later, the plan should still acknowledge this factor early to prepare for
informed decision-making.
Project Road Map and Timelines
Finally, the plan must include a project roadmap that lays out timelines and deliverables. This usually starts
with a pilot project or proof of concept, which is limited in scope to allow the team to learn quickly and achieve
results faster. The roadmap should not only cover this initial pilot but also extend toward long-term goals, such
as scaling RPA to more processes and expanding automation to different departments across the organization.
