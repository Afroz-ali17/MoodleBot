What Is RPA?
1. Origin of the Term RPA
• The term RPA (Robotic Process Automation) was coined in 2012 by Pat Geary,
Chief Evangelist for Blue Prism.
• The category was still evolving at that time, hence the term can feel
slippery/confusing.
2. Breaking Down the Term “RPA”
• Robotic:
o Does not mean a physical robot.
o Refers to a software-based robot (bot) that automates human actions in the
workplace.
o Mainly used in clerical and administrative (white-collar) functions.
o Delivered either via cloud or downloadable software.
o The use of the word "robotic" is partly a savvy marketing move (robots are
seen as “cool”).
• Process:
o Not very descriptive.
o A better word would be “tasks” (individual action items that are part of a
process).
• Automation:
o This part of the term is spot-on.
o It represents the core functionality of RPA: automating tasks.
3. Definition of RPA (In Simple Terms)
• RPA involves bots that perform a set of specified tasks, such as:
o Cut-and-paste of information between applications.
o Opening a website and logging in.
o Opening an email and attachments.
o Reading/writing a database.
o Extracting content from forms/documents.
o Performing calculations and workflows.
• These tasks may seem mundane/boring, but that’s the point:
o RPA focuses on tasks that waste human effort, so employees can do more
important work.
4. How Vendors Define RPA
• UiPath:
o RPA allows anyone to configure software (“robots”) to emulate and
integrate human actions.
o Robots use the UI (user interface) to capture data and manipulate
applications like humans.
o They interpret, trigger responses, communicate with other systems.
o Benefits: Never sleep, make zero mistakes, cost less than employees.
• Automation Anywhere:
o RPA is simple yet powerful.
o Provides tools to create software robots for automating business processes.
o Bots = Digital Workforce.
o Features: configurable, code-free, non-disruptive, scalable, easy to control.
o Bots can interact with systems, learn, and be cloned.
• PEGA:
o RPA = fast, low-risk starting point for automation.
o Useful for processes dependent on legacy systems without APIs.
o Ensures faster and efficient outcomes.
o Limitation:
▪ RPA does not transform an organization by itself.
▪ Not a fix for broken enterprise-wide processes.
▪ For that → requires end-to-end intelligent automation.
• Kryon Systems:
o RPA creates true virtual workforces.
o Virtual workforce = software robots that execute tasks on enterprise apps.
o Managed like a normal team in the organization.
o Virtual workers interact with people like employees.
o Advantages: Faster, more accurate, lower cost.
o Impact: Strong business outcomes + significant ROI.
5. General View
• Vendors provide different definitions and approaches, sometimes starkly
different.
• Common idea: RPA is like having a digital worker.
• Uses technologies like screen scraping and workflows to copy daily employee tasks.
• Fun fact: Some companies even name their bots!
6. RPA vs. Other Automation (e.g., Excel Macros)
• Excel Macros:
o Limited to a particular application.
• RPA:
o Works across multiple systems (PCs, mainframes, etc.).
o Can record human actions to help create automation.
o Some RPA systems use AI (Artificial Intelligence) for advanced capabilities.
o Over time, RPA platforms become a knowledge repository about how work
is done.
o This repository provides insights to improve workflows and processes, driving
further efficiency.

Flavours of RPA
1. Reason for Different Flavours
• RPA has different approaches because:
o The technology is still evolving.
o Vendors try to redefine RPA to stand out in the marketplace.
2. Main Flavours of RPA
On a high level, RPA can be divided into three main types:
(a) Attended RPA
• Also known as: Robotic Desktop Automation (RDA).
• First form of RPA (around 2003).
• Works in collaboration with a human for certain tasks.
• Example:
o Call centre representative speaks to a customer.
o At the same time, RPA software looks up information.
• Best for front-office functions where humans + bots work together.
(b) Unattended RPA
• Second generation of RPA.
• Runs without human involvement.
• Bot is event-triggered (e.g., a customer sends an invoice via email).
• Best suited for back-office functions.
(c) Intelligent Process Automation (IPA)
• Also known as: Cognitive RPA.
• Latest generation of RPA.
• Uses AI (Artificial Intelligence) to:
o Learn over time.
o Interpret documents (e.g., invoices).
o Make decisions using its own insights and judgments.
• Result: Requires less human intervention compared to earlier generations.
3. Importance of Understanding Flavours
• Some RPA systems specialize in a specific approach.
• When adopting automation, organizations should:
o Analyse which type of RPA suits their needs best.

History of RPA
Ancient Roots of Automation
• First mention in Homer’s The Iliad: Hephaestus (Greek god of blacksmiths) used
automatons (machines) to build weapons for the gods.
Modern Development (last 70 years)
• Mainframe Era: IBM’s large, expensive machines. Used for payroll, customer
accounts. Innovators like Ross Perot offered outsourcing for affordability.
• PC Revolution: Intel’s microprocessor + Microsoft’s OS. Allowed automation for all
businesses (word processors, spreadsheets). Drawbacks: complex IT environments,
costly integrations, custom coding, repetitive data moving → sometimes reduced
productivity.
Emergence of RPA (Early 2000s)
• Screen scraping (automating data movement among apps) improved efficiency.
• Early RPA seen as low-tech/commodity. Investors focused more on cloud computing.
RPA Inflection Point (~2012)
• Post-financial crisis: Companies wanted cost-cutting, ERP matured.
• Needed faster digital transformation to avoid disruption from tech firms.
• Banking and regulated industries: reduced paperwork, improved audits, security,
control.
• RPA became more sophisticated, easier to use, higher ROI.
• Large companies started mission-critical adoption.
• Millennials entering workforce: wanted careers and engaging work, not repetitive
tasks.
• Mihir Shukla (CEO, Automation Anywhere): RPA evolution = gradual progress with
periodic breakthroughs (like iPhone after years of incremental innovation).
Present Growth of RPA
• Fastest growing segment in software industry.
• Gartner: 2018 spending $850M (up 63% YoY), forecast $1.3B in 2019.
• Transparency Market Research: Global RPA market projected $5B by 2020.

The Downsides of RPA
• Cost of Ownership: Business models vary (subscription, multiyear license, per bot
charges). Additional costs include training, ongoing maintenance, other required
software/hardware, and third-party consultants for implementation.
• Technical Debt: RPA is not a comprehensive solution. Requires reworking, updates,
and changes as business processes evolve. Bots may fail if processes change, so
continuous attention is needed.
• Enterprise Scale: Scaling RPA across the organization is difficult. Managing
numerous bots and ensuring IT collaboration is challenging. Deloitte UK study: Only
3% of organizations scaled RPA to 50+ bots.
• Security: Growing risk as RPA covers mission-critical processes. A breach can
expose sensitive data. In manufacturing, risks may include property damage or bodily
harm, especially with attended RPA.
• Expectations: Hype leads to unrealistic expectations. PEGA survey: Average time to
develop a quality bot = 18 months; only 39% deployed on time.
• Preparation: Without analysing current tasks deeply, RPA may automate bad
processes, leading to inefficiency.
• Limits: RPA mainly handles routine, repetitive tasks. Judgment-based tasks
(approvals, verifications) still need humans. With AI integration, these limitations
may reduce (e.g., insurance claim adjudication).
• Virtualized Environments: RPA may fail in remote-access platforms (e.g., Citrix)
since it cannot capture on-screen text. Latest RPA tools (like UiPath) are addressing
this issue.
RPA Compared to BPO, BPM, and BPA
1. BPM (Business Process Management)
• Origin: Emerged in response to global competition (1970s–1980s). US companies
adopted Six Sigma, Lean Production (Toyota principles), TQM (blend of Six
Sigma + Lean), and computer technologies like FileNet (digital workflow, later
acquired by IBM). ERP vendors (e.g., PeopleSoft) also emerged.
• BPM = comprehensive improvement of business processes (optimizing both
employee systems + IT assets).
• Business Process Management Software (BPMS) like Laserfiche (founded 1987 by
Nien-Ling Wacker using OCR for document search).
• Difference from RPA:
o BPM = changing entire processes, requires detailed documentation, training,
and takes more time/effort.
o RPA = automates tasks, quicker, less effort.
• BPM attractive for heavily regulated industries (financial services, healthcare). Risk:
too much structure may stifle innovation.
• RPA is complementary to BPM → BPM improves processes, RPA fills gaps.
• UiPath analogy:
o BPM = rebuilding roads for self-driving cars.
o RPA = driving existing cars like humans.
o Both needed, depending on situation.
2. BPO (Business Process Outsourcing)
• Definition: Outsourcing business functions (payroll, HR, procurement, customer
support).
• Market Size: Forecasted revenue $343.2B by 2025 (Grand View Research).
• Top Players: ADP, Accenture, Infosys, IBM, TCS, Cognizant.
• Advantages:
o Lower wages in other countries (labour arbitrage).
o Access to educated, multilingual workforce.
o Focus on core business while specialists handle non-core functions.
• Types:
o Offshore – employees in distant countries.
o Nearshore – neighbouring country, higher cost but easier collaboration.
o Onshore – same country, but wage differences apply.
• Drawbacks:
o Quality issues (e.g., poor communication with call center agents).
o Security risks when handling sensitive company data.
o Rising labor costs in countries like China & India → relocation disruptions.
o Political instability can disrupt BPO operations.
• RPA Connection:
o RPA automates BPO-type activities.
o Studies (Everest Group, KPMG, Deloitte): RPA offers up to 70% cost
advantage vs outsourcing.
o BPO firms themselves are heavy users of RPA to stay competitive.
3. BPA (Business Process Automation)
• Definition: Use of technology to automate a complete process.
• Example: Employee Onboarding – multiple repeatable steps with paperwork → BPA
streamlines at scale.
• Difference from RPA:
o RPA = automates part of a process (specific tasks).
o BPA = automates entire process end-to-end.

The Workforce of the Future
The modern concept of work can be traced back to Henry Ford, who revolutionized the
automotive industry by scaling operations and introducing new policies. To attract the best
employees, he doubled wages to $5 a day, which expanded the middle class. He also
introduced the 40-hour workweek with weekends off, setting a foundation for modern work
culture.
Since then, the fundamentals of work have not changed much. Although the gig economy
(services through Uber, Lyft, etc.) has emerged, the structure of office work remains durable.
However, many activities are still tedious and repetitive. According to McKinsey Global
Institute, white-collar workers spend 60% of their time on manual tasks like emails,
spreadsheets, notes, and calls. Inefficient processes waste nearly $575 billion annually in the
US (David White).
Here, Robotic Process Automation (RPA) is expected to have a significant impact because
repetitive processes will be automated. A concern is job loss: a Forrester survey (2025)
predicts that 9% of the world’s jobs (230 million) will be lost due to software automation.
The study also estimates 16% job replacement in the US with only 9% new jobs created.
Similarly, McKinsey & Company analysis shows that technologies like RPA could
automate 45% of workforce activities.
Interestingly, CEOs usually avoid directly addressing job losses during automation projects,
since it may cause fear and negative headlines. Instead, they highlight the overall benefits of
transformation. But in reality, the impact will grow significantly as RPA systems become
more robust. History shows that whenever technology causes job losses (e.g., Industrial
Revolution), it leads to political and regulatory changes, and sometimes technology becomes
a target of criticism.
Although companies generally avoid layoffs because they are costly and harmful to
organizations, managers will increasingly need to reskill employees and find new roles to
handle automation-driven changes.
Overall, automation has the potential to create a better society, where workers focus on
interesting and engaging activities instead of repetitive tasks. It will also encourage
continuous renewal of knowledge and skills. However, proactive efforts from both
companies and governments are necessary to ensure a smoother transition.
A survey by PEGA shows that nearly 70% of people believe the future workforce will
consist of both humans and machines, and 88% are comfortable with this, as long as they
are not managed by machines.

RPA Skills
RPA does not require deep programming skills, but there is a strong need to understand
certain high-level technology concepts. The technological ecosystem often appears
overwhelming because of the large number of technical acronyms such as ACL, API, OCR,
CPU, HTTP, IP, JSON, NOC, PCI, RAM, and SaaS. Even professionals with years of
experience in the technology industry may not know many of these terms in detail or may
only have a vague understanding of their meanings. For instance, Kubernetes, also referred to
as K8s, is defined as an open-source system designed to automate deployment, scaling, and
management of containerized applications. However, this definition itself is quite complex
and requires a deep knowledge of computer systems and software architecture to fully grasp.
Although the world of technology is filled with such complex terminologies and frameworks,
the encouraging fact is that for using Robotic Process Automation effectively, one does not
need to master all these advanced or highly technical concepts. Instead, there are only a
handful of important terms and concepts that need to be understood in order to work with
RPA successfully. These essential concepts form the foundation of practical knowledge that
professionals should focus on, rather than attempting to become experts in every aspect of
software or systems architecture.
Thus, the key point is that while RPA operates in a technology-rich environment filled with
acronyms and technical jargon, it does not demand deep technical specialization. A learner or
practitioner only has to develop familiarity with a select set of terms that directly relate to the
working and deployment of RPA tools. This makes RPA accessible to non-programmers and
business users, opening the door for wider adoption of automation in various industries.

On-Premise vs. the Cloud
Introduction
The deployment of technology in organizations has traditionally revolved around two major
approaches: on-premise systems and cloud computing. Both models have their advantages
and limitations, and understanding them is crucial for implementing automation technologies
such as RPA, which often depend on the underlying infrastructure.
On-Premise Systems
On-premise technology refers to a model where a company purchases and sets up its own
hardware and software within its own data centre. The benefits of this approach include
complete control over data, systems, and processes, which is especially important for highly
regulated industries requiring high levels of security and privacy. Another advantage is the
ability to customize solutions based on unique company needs and IT policies. However, on-
premise systems have significant drawbacks. The cost of ownership is one of the biggest
challenges since it involves heavy up-front capital investment along with continuous
expenses for maintenance, upgrades, and monitoring. IT departments also spend much of
their time managing non-core activities rather than focusing on innovation. Additionally,
point applications such as Excel may result in fragmented environments where data becomes
scattered and difficult to centralize.
Evolution of Cloud Computing
Cloud computing emerged as a response to the limitations of on-premise systems.
Interestingly, the concept of cloud traces back to the late 1950s with John McCarthy’s
invention of time-sharing, which allowed multiple users to access mainframes. However,
during the 1980s and 1990s, the on-premise model became dominant because of the
widespread adoption of PCs, while the Internet was still underdeveloped and limited to the
military, universities, and large organizations. As the Internet matured, cloud computing
gained momentum. One of the pioneering examples was Salesforce.com, which offered
business applications directly through a browser under the Software-as-a-Service (SaaS)
model. According to Marc Benioff, the cofounder and CEO of Salesforce, the vision was to
simplify software delivery, eliminate the need for complex installations, and replace
multimillion-dollar software packages with pay-per-user, per-month services delivered
instantly via the Internet. Although initially challenging to implement, Salesforce.com grew
into the dominant cloud applications company, with revenues of more than $13 billion in
2020 and a market capitalization of $131 billion.
Advantages and Disadvantages of Cloud Computing
The cloud model offers several benefits. It eliminates heavy up-front costs, provides
scalability, reduces the burden of maintenance, and allows quick deployment of software.
However, there are downsides as well. Since companies have less control over the platform,
there are vulnerabilities related to security and privacy. Outages are another major concern,
as they can cause significant disruption. Cloud services are also not always cheap, with
Salesforce.com often criticized for high costs. Nevertheless, cloud computing continues to
expand rapidly. Gartner predicted that the global market for public cloud services would
grow from $214.3 billion in 2019 to $331.2 billion in 2022, almost triple the growth rate of
overall IT services.
Amazon Web Services and Other Platforms
A major turning point in cloud evolution was the launch of Amazon Web Services (AWS) in
2005. AWS provided a platform that enabled companies to build cloud-native applications
while handling complex infrastructure requirements such as storage, security, compute,
database access, analytics, IoT, and deployment. This simplified the development of
applications, reduced costs, and introduced a per-use pricing model. The scale of AWS
adoption has been massive, with companies like Slack planning to spend $250 million and
Pinterest allocating $750 million over several years. Other technology giants such as
Microsoft and Google developed their own platforms, Azure and Google Cloud, respectively.
Many companies now adopt multi-cloud strategies to ensure redundancy and flexibility.
Types of Cloud Models
Cloud computing can be categorized into different approaches. Public cloud refers to services
accessed from remote servers owned by providers like AWS, Salesforce, and Microsoft.
These services operate on a multitenant architecture that allows secure sharing of large
infrastructures, making them cost-effective. Private cloud refers to a model where a company
owns and manages its own data center, giving it greater control and security but at the
expense of scale and cost advantages. Hybrid cloud combines both models, with the public
cloud handling less critical functions and the private cloud managing sensitive processes.
Implications for RPA
For RPA, the choice between on-premise and cloud environments carries significant
implications. RPA platforms traditionally started as on-premise solutions and only recently
began transitioning to the cloud. The challenge arises because RPA requires deep integration
across multiple applications and environments, making it more difficult to design cloud-
native systems. In some cases, on-premise RPA systems may be hosted on cloud platforms
like AWS, but these are not truly cloud-native since they still require upgrades and
maintenance. Therefore, the evolution of cloud-native RPA systems is ongoing and remains a
critical factor in the future of automation.

Web Technology
Introduction
The development of the World Wide Web marked a turning point in communication and
information sharing. It was created by British scientist Tim Berners-Lee in 1990. His main
contribution was the use of hyperlinks that allowed users to navigate across different web
pages. Although the foundation was laid in 1990, the real growth of the web came in the mid-
1990s with the launch of the Netscape browser, which triggered the Internet revolution.
HTML and Its Role
At the core of web technology was HTML, or Hypertext Markup Language. HTML consists
of a set of commands and tags used to display text, apply colors, and present graphics on a
web page. Its simplicity was the main reason for the rapid spread of websites, as even
beginners could learn and use it easily. For example, commands in HTML work by
surrounding content with tags such as <strong>This is a Title</strong> which results in bold
text. This made it possible for non-technical users to create web pages quickly.
Limitations of HTML
While HTML was effective in its early days, it was limited in design and functionality. As the
demand for more interactive and visually appealing websites grew, it became clear that
HTML alone was not sufficient. It lacked advanced styling, design flexibility, and the ability
to handle complex tasks beyond simple text and graphics display.
Emergence of CSS
To overcome these limitations, CSS or Cascading Style Sheets were introduced. CSS allows
developers to control the look and feel of a website. It can define colors, borders, shadows,
fonts, spacing, and even create animations. CSS separates the structure (HTML) from the
presentation, which made web design more organized and professional. It also allowed
multiple pages to share a consistent design without rewriting the same formatting rules.
Role of JavaScript
Another key advancement was the introduction of JavaScript. JavaScript added interactivity
and functionality to websites, enabling actions such as validating forms, performing
calculations, and creating dynamic content without reloading a page. It made websites more
engaging, user-friendly, and capable of handling sophisticated tasks. Over time, JavaScript
evolved into a powerful programming language used for front-end as well as back-end
development.
Relevance to RPA
Robotic Process Automation (RPA) interacts with web technologies to perform automation
tasks. Since many business applications run on web-based systems, RPA needs to recognize
and interact with HTML tags, CSS styles, and JavaScript elements to carry out actions like
filling forms, extracting data, and clicking buttons. Understanding these technologies helps in
building more reliable automation processes.

Programming Languages and Low Code
Introduction to Programming Languages
A programming language is a formal way of giving instructions to a computer so that it can
perform specific tasks. These instructions are written in a structured format that the computer
can understand and execute. Programming languages generally use simple and meaningful
words like IF, DO, WHILE, and THEN to make logical decisions and control the flow of
operations. However, beyond these simple commands, programming can become complex,
especially when dealing with advanced concepts like object-oriented programming (OOP).
OOP introduces features such as classes, objects, inheritance, and polymorphism, which
allow for building scalable and reusable programs but also add to the complexity of coding.
Popular Programming Languages
In the present digital world, some programming languages dominate because of their
efficiency, community support, and versatility. Python is highly popular for its simplicity
and extensive libraries in data science, artificial intelligence, and automation. Java is widely
used in enterprise applications, mobile app development, and large-scale systems. C++ is
known for performance-heavy applications like gaming, simulations, and embedded systems.
C# is the backbone of Microsoft’s .NET framework, suitable for building desktop and web
applications. Ruby is valued for its elegant syntax and is often used in web development with
the Ruby on Rails framework. These languages form the backbone of modern software and
application development.
Programming in RPA Systems
When it comes to Robotic Process Automation (RPA), programming is required but not at
the same depth as traditional software development. RPA platforms are designed to simplify
the coding effort and allow users to automate workflows without writing extensive code. This
is where the concept of low code comes in.
Low Code Approach
Low code is a method where automation can be created with minimal manual coding.
Instead of writing long scripts, RPA systems provide drag-and-drop tools, flowcharts, and
visual designers that allow the user to build bots in a more intuitive way. For example,
instead of coding an instruction to open an application and copy data, the user can select
ready-made components from a toolbox and connect them visually to define the workflow.
This reduces the learning curve for non-programmers and speeds up automation
development.
Training and Understanding Workflows
Although low code reduces complexity, it does not mean there is no learning involved. To
use low code efficiently, one must still understand workflow structures, process logic, and
decision-making steps. This includes concepts like loops (repeated actions), conditions (IF
statements), and exception handling (managing errors). A clear understanding of business
processes is also essential because RPA bots need to replicate the steps followed by humans
in an organization.
Summary
Thus, programming languages form the base for instructing computers, while low code in
RPA simplifies automation development by using visual tools and minimal manual coding.
However, proper knowledge of workflows and logical structures remains necessary for
effective use of low code platforms.

OCR (Optical Character Recognition)
Introduction to OCR
OCR (Optical Character Recognition) is a crucial feature in RPA platforms, designed to
convert printed, scanned, or handwritten documents into machine-readable text. It essentially
involves two parts: a scanner device (such as a document scanner or even a smartphone
camera) and specialized software that can analyze and recognize the text. By doing this, OCR
allows images, PDFs, and paper-based content to be converted into editable and searchable
data, making it highly useful for automation tasks.
Challenges in OCR Accuracy
OCR, while powerful, has historically faced many challenges that affect its accuracy. These
challenges include:
• Font size issues where very small or large text becomes difficult to recognize.
• Shape of the text such as decorative or unusual fonts that make recognition harder.
• Skewness of documents where text may be rotated or slanted, confusing the OCR
engine.
• Blurred or degraded text often seen in old documents or low-quality scans.
• Background noise such as smudges, stains, or overlapping marks.
• Language diversity since OCR must adapt to different writing systems and scripts.
Advancements in OCR Technology
In the early years, OCR systems were far from accurate and often produced errors. However,
advances in AI algorithms, fuzzy logic, and more powerful computing hardware have
dramatically improved OCR’s performance. Modern OCR solutions can now achieve
accuracy rates close to 100% in many cases, making them reliable for critical business
applications.
OCR in RPA Workflows
Within RPA, OCR plays a transformative role by enabling bots to recognize and interact with
text-based information across multiple applications. For instance, when recording a person’s
actions on an application, OCR enhances the bot’s ability to capture words and visuals on the
screen. This ensures that even if the layout or location of elements changes, the RPA system
can still identify them correctly, thereby maintaining the workflow.
Document Processing with OCR in RPA
Automation often deals with large numbers of documents, making OCR indispensable for
speeding up processing. A practical example is loan processing:
• OCR extracts data such as a person’s financial background, property details, and other
required information.
• The RPA system then applies business rules and workflows, such as risk assessment,
compliance checks, and routing documents to relevant departments or regulatory
agencies.
This process greatly reduces manual effort, improves accuracy, and accelerates
turnaround times.
Limitations of Built-in OCR in RPA Systems
Although many RPA systems include their own OCR capabilities, these are not always
sufficient for industries heavily dependent on handwritten or complex documents. Sectors
like healthcare, insurance, government, and banking still rely on forms that are time-
consuming and difficult to process with standard OCR tools.
Advanced OCR Solutions for RPA
To address these limitations, advanced OCR systems like HyperScience have emerged.
HyperScience uses machine learning (ML) algorithms to handle complex recognition tasks,
including cursive handwriting, structured field detection in invoices, and data reconciliation.
Such systems are highly efficient, with the ability to automate up to 90% of document
processing, significantly reducing costs and improving efficiency.

DATABASES
Introduction
At the core of most modern applications lies the concept of a database, which is essentially a
system designed to store data that can later be searched, updated, and retrieved as needed.
Typically, this data is organized into tables consisting of rows and columns, providing a
structured way of handling information. Among the different types of databases, the
relational database has emerged as the most dominant form since its invention in 1970 by
IBM researcher E. F. Codd. This model is based on structured data and is accessed through
SQL (Structured Query Language), a scripting language designed to be relatively easy to
learn and use.
Commercialization of Relational Databases
Although relational databases were proposed in the early 1970s, their commercialization
began in the late 1970s, with Oracle leading the pioneering efforts. Following Oracle’s
success, several start-ups also entered the field to capitalize on this opportunity. By the late
1980s, the enterprise market was dominated by Oracle and SAP, while Microsoft became
the standard choice for the mid-market segment. This marked the era of widespread adoption
of relational databases across industries.
Challenges with Relational Databases
Despite their effectiveness, relational databases came with certain limitations that created
long-term challenges for organizations. One major issue was data sprawl, which refers to the
uncontrollable growth and proliferation of tables across an organization. This fragmentation
made it difficult to centralize data, thereby preventing businesses from obtaining a holistic,
360-degree view of their information. Another challenge was the high cost of relational
databases, making them less feasible for smaller organizations. Furthermore, with the rise of
cloud computing, real-time applications, and mobile technologies, relational databases
struggled to efficiently handle the increasing demands for data processing and scalability.
Emergence of Alternatives: Data Warehousing and Hadoop
In response to these challenges, alternative approaches to data management emerged. One
key innovation was the data warehouse, which began as an open-source project in the late
1990s by Doug Cutting. This effort eventually evolved into Hadoop, a technology capable
of handling massive volumes of Big Data. Yahoo! was one of the first major adopters of
Hadoop, using it to manage the data demands of its large-scale digital platforms. Following
Yahoo!, companies such as Facebook and Twitter also adopted Hadoop. The core
advantage of data warehouses was that they enabled organizations to achieve a
comprehensive, 360-degree view of their data, making large-scale analytics feasible. Over
time, investments from companies such as Google, Amazon, and Microsoft further
advanced data warehouse systems. Additionally, innovative start-ups like Snowflake have
entered the market, pushing new boundaries in efficiency, scalability, and integration.
New Approaches to Databases
Alongside data warehouses, new open-source relational systems such as MySQL (now
owned by Oracle) and PostgreSQL also emerged. However, despite their technical strengths,
these systems did not achieve significant traction within large enterprises. The real disruption
came from the rise of next-generation NoSQL databases.
NoSQL Databases
The NoSQL model represented a dramatic departure from traditional relational approaches.
Initially launched as an open-source project, NoSQL rapidly gained momentum. A notable
example is MongoDB, which has seen adoption from 14,200 customers across 100
countries, with more than 70 million downloads worldwide. Unlike relational databases that
enforce rigid structures, NoSQL offers flexibility through its document model, making it
highly scalable and suitable for handling petabyte-scale data. This adaptability has allowed
NoSQL databases to thrive in environments requiring massive, unstructured, or semi-
structured data management.
Databases in the Cloud
Another major trend reshaping the database industry is the migration of databases to the
cloud. According to research from Gartner, about 75% of databases are expected to be
migrated to cloud platforms. The shift to the cloud provides organizations with several
advantages, including easier scalability, cost efficiency, and enhanced capabilities for
analytics and Artificial Intelligence (AI) integration. Cloud-based databases also reduce
infrastructure management overheads, making them an attractive choice for businesses of all
sizes.
Future Outlook
Looking ahead, database technology is set to witness continuous innovation, driven by
cloud platforms, AI integration, and the need to manage massive, complex datasets.
Nevertheless, relational databases will continue to remain the majority choice for
companies, which means that Robotic Process Automation (RPA) will continue to interact
predominantly with relational systems. However, the growing importance of NoSQL, cloud
databases, and advanced data warehouses indicates a hybrid database environment, where
different types of databases coexist to meet diverse organizational needs.

APIs (Application Programming Interfaces)
Definition and Basic Concept
An API (Application Programming Interface) is software that connects two applications
together and allows them to communicate. It acts like a bridge between systems. For
example, if you want to create a weather app, instead of manually collecting data, you can set
up an API that fetches weather information from an external source. Setting up an API is
often straightforward and can be done with just a few lines of code to request data such as the
weather for a particular city. This approach increases the speed of development and makes
the process much more efficient.
Prevalence and Types of APIs
APIs are widely used in enterprise environments because of their effectiveness in
connecting different systems. They can have different structures, but the most common type
is the REST (Representational State Transfer) API, which has become a standard for
building web-based integrations due to its simplicity, scalability, and efficiency.
Use of APIs in Automation
APIs can also function as a form of automation since they enable applications to work
together without manual effort. However, using APIs for automation requires some important
considerations.
Challenges and Considerations
1. Technical Expertise Requirement – Developing and maintaining APIs requires
people with technical backgrounds who understand coding and integration concepts.
2. Development Complexity and Time – Building an API can take time and involve
complex integration processes. This includes not just development but also ongoing
testing to ensure the system works correctly. Although, third-party services are
available that can simplify this process.
3. Maintenance Issues – APIs must be maintained regularly. It is common for an API to
break when the structure changes, meaning developers need to continuously
monitor and update it.
4. Limitations of Off-the-Shelf APIs – Even if a ready-made API is available, there are
still challenges. One major issue is metering, where the API usage may be limited to
a certain number of requests per day or hour, unless higher pricing is paid.
Additionally, APIs can still face bugs and glitches, especially in complex IT
environments, leading to reliability concerns.
RPA as an Alternative to APIs
Due to these difficulties, Robotic Process Automation (RPA) has become an attractive
alternative. With RPA, the development is easier, and there is less need for complex
integrations compared to APIs. Interestingly, RPA platforms themselves can also act as
vehicles for delivering advanced APIs within enterprises, creating a combination of
automation power and integration capability.

Agile, Scrum, Kanban, and Waterfall
Complexity of Software Development
Software development is inherently complex, not only because of the technical challenges but
also due to the need to manage teams that may be spread across different countries.
Moreover, technologies continue to evolve, and maintaining a system often requires adding
new features and upgrading underlying components. Even top-tier companies face
difficulties; for instance, Microsoft’s Vista operating system took five years to develop. This
delay was caused by organizational silos, poor collaboration, and a lack of system-wide
integration. Teams often built efficient pieces of code that failed to function properly within
the larger system, leading to slow development and persistent mishaps.
The Need for Software Management Approaches
With new platforms such as the cloud and hybrid cloud making software development even
more difficult, structured software management approaches have become essential.
Agile Methodology
Agile was created in the 1990s and was formally shaped by the Manifesto for Agile
Software Development. The main focus of Agile is incremental and iterative
development, beginning with a clear plan but relying on constant collaboration and
communication among teams, including people from the business side. Modern collaboration
tools such as Slack and Zoom have made Agile easier to adopt, reducing reliance on emails.
For example, Tim Tully, CTO of Splunk, highlighted how his email volume declined
substantially because his teams primarily collaborate through Slack.
Scrum
Scrum is a subset of Agile that emphasizes short, iterative cycles called sprints, typically
lasting one to two weeks. This approach maintains project momentum and helps break down
large projects into manageable portions. Interestingly, Scrum was initially used in
manufacturing before being adapted successfully to software development.
Kanban
Kanban, a term derived from the Japanese word for “visual sign” or “card,” originates from
Toyota’s high-quality manufacturing processes. The system uses visual tools to streamline
workflows and is similar to Agile in its reliance on iterative development. Kanban provides
transparency and efficiency by making project progress and bottlenecks easily visible.
Waterfall
Waterfall is the traditional software development model dating back to the 1970s. It
follows a highly structured, step-by-step plan with extensive documentation. Tools like
Gantt charts are often used to track progress. While Waterfall has the advantage of detailed
planning, it has lost popularity due to several drawbacks: difficulty in making changes,
tedious processes, and a high risk of delays.
