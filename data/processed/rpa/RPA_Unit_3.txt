RPA Vendor Evaluation
The Right Factors to Consider
When it comes to Robotic Process Automation (RPA), one of the biggest challenges organizations face is the
selection of the right vendor. The market itself is still in the early phases and continues to evolve rapidly,
particularly with the integration of emerging technologies like Artificial Intelligence (AI). This evolving
nature of the industry makes vendor evaluation an essential step.
The Size of the Market
Currently, there are over 70 software vendors offering RPA solutions. The high number of players is due to
several reasons:
• The unclear and fuzzy definition of RPA, which allows different interpretations and varied offerings.
• The surge in funding and investments in the RPA industry, which has encouraged many entrepreneurs
to establish new start-ups.
• The relative ease of building RPA software, especially for basic functionalities like screen scraping,
has lowered the barrier to entry for vendors.
Why Evaluation Is Challenging
Given the profusion of vendors, conducting a comprehensive evaluation of all providers is unpractical and
unrealistic. The sheer variety of tools, features, and capabilities makes it impossible to review each one in
depth.
The Practical Approach
Instead of attempting to analyze every vendor, organizations need to develop a short list of potential vendors.
This short list should be based on how well the offerings fit the specific requirements of the organization. The
aim is to filter out irrelevant players and focus on those vendors that align closely with business goals, process
needs, and scalability requirements.
What This Chapter Covers
This chapter focuses on providing guidance on the key factors involved in making the right selection of an
RPA vendor. By understanding these factors, decision-makers can narrow down their choices effectively and
ensure the selected vendor is capable of supporting both current automation needs and future growth
opportunities.

Minimum Capabilities
Misuse of the RPA Label
With the growing fervent interest in RPA, many software companies have begun to label themselves as RPA
providers, even when their offerings have little or no real connection to true RPA functionalities. This
practice is not new in the software industry but can lead to terrible results for customers who invest in tools
that fail to meet the expected standards.
Core RPA Functions to Look For
To avoid falling into this trap, it is essential to verify that any potential software vendor provides the minimum
core RPA capabilities. At the very least, this includes:
• Creation and deployment of bots to carry out automation tasks.
• Screen scraping functionality, which forms a key part of traditional RPA.
• Built-in integrations for common applications, ensuring that bots can interact seamlessly with widely
used business systems.
• A robust management console for bots that enables tracking, orchestration, and analytics to monitor
and optimize performance.
• Mechanisms for handling business exceptions, where human intervention becomes necessary when a
bot fails or encounters a scenario it cannot process.
Proof of Concept (POC) as a First Stage
The first stage of the evaluation process should include running a Proof of Concept (POC). Since most RPA
vendors provide trial periods, organizations should use this opportunity to launch a small project. This
practical test helps assess the features, capabilities, and limitations of the software before making a larger
commitment.
POC vs. Pilot Program
It is important not to confuse a POC with a pilot program, as the two serve different purposes:
• A POC usually occurs in the early stages within a development environment. It is designed to test
feasibility and explore whether the tool meets the required basic needs.
• A pilot program, on the other hand, is applied to a real-world application intended for production
use. It comes after the POC stage and serves as a bridge toward full-scale deployment.

Who Is the User?
The Importance of Considering the End User
At first glance, the question of who the user is might seem overly simplistic. However, in reality, this factor is
often overlooked during the decision-making process for purchasing RPA software. Ignoring the needs of the
end user can create a serious risk of poor adoption, where the system fails to gain traction within the
organization.
Selecting the Right Tool for the Right Audience
According to Niraj Patel, Managing Director of Artificial Intelligence at DMI, the choice of RPA software
must align with the intended audience. For example:
• Front-end RPA: This type connects consumers directly to bots. Here, organizations must select
conversational technologies. Additionally, for front-end scenarios, it may be beneficial to designate
call center agents as bot owners, enabling them to measure, monitor, and optimize bot
performance.
• Back-end RPA: This type is more system-oriented and deals with core operations. It should be
managed by technical personnel who can evaluate the quality of work performed by the bots.
The Role of Non-Technical End Users
In most cases, the end users of RPA software are not technical personnel. Instead, they are typically
administrative staff or business professionals. This means the software must be user-friendly, intuitive, and
accessible to those without advanced IT skills.
Active Business Participation vs. Traditional IT Ownership
As David Lee, Senior Research Analyst of Applications – Data and Analytics at Info-Tech Research Group,
highlights, there is a critical difference between RPA vendor evaluation and traditional enterprise software
evaluation, such as ERP systems. In ERP selection, IT teams typically take the lead in testing and
recommending solutions. However, in RPA:
• Business users must play an active role in vendor evaluation.
• IT cannot be the sole tester of bot creation; instead, business teams must build and test bots
themselves, since they are the ones who truly understand the processes to be automated.
• Too often, businesses mistakenly treat RPA as a purely technical solution, handing it off to IT and
expecting them to return with a final recommendation. This approach fails, because RPA’s success
depends on direct business user engagement in the evaluation and implementation stages.

Funding
Venture Funding Trends
In the first half of 2019, venture funding in the United States remained extremely strong, reaching $62 billion
in investments for start-ups. If this pace were to continue, it would result in an all-time record for the year.
Similarly, foreign markets demonstrated strength, generating approximately $104 billion in funding.
Concentration of Investments
Despite these large figures, the reality is that funding markets concentrate on a relatively small number of
deals. This is because the investment amounts per deal have grown significantly. As a result, even if a
category like RPA is red hot and highly attractive, not every vendor will be able to secure sufficient
funding.
Implications for Vendor Evaluation
When evaluating an RPA vendor, funding strength is a critical factor to consider. Several key questions arise:
• Does the vendor have enough resources to invest in research and development (R&D)? Without
this, the company may fall behind in innovation and fail to deliver advanced features.
• Will the vendor be able to build and maintain the necessary infrastructure and support systems?
Strong funding is essential for scalability and long-term stability.
• Can a small start-up compete in the talent market? With large, well-funded players dominating,
smaller vendors may struggle to attract and retain top talent in engineering, AI, and automation.
• Does the vendor have the financial capability to pursue acquisitions? In a competitive space like
RPA, mergers and acquisitions are common strategies to expand capabilities, strengthen market share,
and stay competitive.

Ecosystem
Limitations of RPA Vendors
Even the largest RPA players in the market lack the resources to create a true end-to-end solution on their
own. This means that, in most cases, customers will inevitably require additional software to address gaps
within the RPA system. In fact, some of these gaps can be quite significant, making external support and
complementary tools necessary.
Importance of a Thriving Ecosystem
Because of these limitations, it is essential to select an RPA platform that offers a robust and thriving
ecosystem of partners and integrations. A strong ecosystem ensures that organizations can access the
necessary complementary technologies and accelerate the process of filling in missing capabilities.
The Role of a Bot Store
One of the most important indicators of a healthy ecosystem is the presence of a bot store, which functions like
an app store for automation bots and components. A bot store provides ready-made bots that can be
downloaded and deployed quickly, significantly reducing the need for custom development.
Benefits of a Bot Store
The availability of pre-built bots and reusable components leads to:
• Reduced development time, as organizations do not need to build everything from scratch.
• Improved performance, since bots from the ecosystem are often optimized and tested.
• Cost savings, as organizations benefit from faster automation cycles. For instance, Automation
Anywhere has reported that its Bot Store has delivered cost savings of 50% to 70% through
accelerated process automation.

Costs
The Central Role of Costs in RPA Selection
When organizations evaluate RPA platforms, costs are usually the most top-of-mind factor. However,
placing too much focus on cost alone can lead to the selection of the wrong platform, which could severely
hamper the success of the project. While RPA vendor pricing is highly competitive, the main consideration
should always be whether the software can effectively solve the organization’s problems.
Hidden Minefields in RPA Costs
Despite competitive pricing, RPA adoption can involve notable financial pitfalls:
• Some platforms require significant consulting costs during setup, sometimes two to three times
higher than the software costs.
• For large enterprises, consulting expenses can easily reach multimillion-dollar figures.
• While these investments are not necessarily wasted—especially when reengineering is required—the
service providers must clearly communicate expectations upfront to avoid surprises.
Factors to Consider in Software Costs
1. Scale and ROI
o Many RPA projects begin on a small scale and are not yet widespread across organizations.
o Businesses must analyze the cost of scaling the technology and determine whether the ROI will
justify expansion.
o This requires modeling large deployment scenarios, factoring in not just software costs but also
consulting, hardware, and server requirements, which often escalate as the project scales.
o Evaluations will reveal significant cost discrepancies among vendors. Higher costs, however,
should not always be seen as a deal-breaker, since they may be leveraged during negotiations
for volume discounts or multi-year agreements.
2. Nonhuman RPA Access Costs
o An emerging cost challenge involves new pricing metrics introduced by some vendors.
o According to Thomas Phelps, vendors may charge for nonhuman access or transactions
performed by the system to compensate for declining revenues from user-based
subscriptions.
o This hidden pricing model could increase long-term costs unexpectedly if not addressed during
negotiations.
How Pricing Models Typically Work
Although pricing varies across vendors, the general structure usually includes:
• Annual payments for the studio/bot designer and the orchestration system, often exceeding $20,000
per year.
• The main cost driver is the bots themselves, with most vendors charging on a per-bot basis.
o Attended bots: Approximately $1,000 to $2,000 each per year.
o Unattended bots: Much higher, typically $5,000 to $10,000 each per year.
Complexity of Per-Bot Pricing
The per-bot pricing model is far from straightforward because bots differ widely across software platforms.
This makes true comparisons difficult. Organizations should therefore ask detailed questions to understand
what they are really paying for. This line of inquiry can also provide an opportunity to negotiate lower prices,
especially if rival vendors offer stronger or cheaper features.
Alternative Pricing Models
While the per-bot model dominates the RPA market, some vendors—particularly those closer to the business
process management (BPM) market—may use other models, such as:
• Input-based pricing, which charges based on the number of full-time equivalent (FTE) employees or
time and materials.
• An output-based model, which charges based on the volume of transactions processed.
Still, despite these alternatives, the per-bot pricing model remains the primary approach across most of the
RPA industry.

Training and Education in RPA Evaluation
Taking Courses as a Measure of Usability
One effective way to evaluate any RPA system is by participating in the training courses offered by the vendor.
These courses help determine whether the software is easy to use and whether it provides the essential features
required for automation tasks. By going through such structured training, organizations and individuals can
gauge the level of complexity, user-friendliness, and practical applicability of the platform.
Free Downloads and Community Editions
Many RPA vendors also offer free downloads of their software or provide community editions. These options
are highly valuable for organizations and individuals as they allow hands-on experimentation without initial
investment. Testing through these editions helps in understanding the system’s capabilities, adaptability, and
performance before committing to large-scale adoption.
Importance of Online Forums and Community Support
Evaluating an RPA system also involves exploring its online forums and community engagement. It is
important to check whether there is a thriving, active community around the software. If there is consistent
participation, it indicates that users are actively engaged, sharing solutions, and helping each other. On the other
hand, if the forums have little to no recent activity, or if the majority of posts are filled with complaints about
the software, this is a red flag. A vibrant and supportive community adds significant value as it becomes a
source of troubleshooting, learning, and innovation.

Support
When selecting an RPA vendor, one of the most critical factors to evaluate is the level and quality of support
they provide. A reliable RPA vendor should offer comprehensive self-service options such as videos, forums,
courses, white papers, documentation, and blogs. These resources ensure that users can independently resolve
common challenges, learn at their own pace, and continuously improve their RPA knowledge. Beyond self-
service, the vendor’s technical support capabilities must also be assessed. Important considerations include
whether the support team can be contacted 24/7 and the responsiveness of their issue resolution. Additionally,
the Service-Level Agreement (SLA) plays a crucial role. The SLA is a formal contract that clearly defines the
level of service, response times, and commitments the vendor guarantees, giving businesses clarity and
assurance regarding the reliability of their automation platform.

Best-of-Breed vs. End-to-End
Another major consideration in RPA adoption is the choice between best-of-breed and end-to-end solutions.
An end-to-end solution may seem attractive because it typically requires less training and is often more cost-
effective in implementation. However, the downside is that certain features may be weak or subpar, as even
leading global technology companies struggle to deliver platforms without gaps or deficiencies. In contrast,
best-of-breed solutions focus on excelling in specialized functions but may require integration with other tools
to achieve full coverage. In the RPA space, achieving a true end-to-end solution is often unrealistic,
especially for large global enterprises with complex and diverse automation needs. Many such organizations
often use multiple RPA platforms simultaneously to address their requirements. To overcome limitations, it
is advisable to prioritize platforms with a robust ecosystem of partners and integrations. Such ecosystems
allow businesses to extend RPA capabilities through complementary technologies like Optical Character
Recognition (OCR), Machine Learning (ML), and Natural Language Processing (NLP), ensuring that the
automation framework is both versatile and scalable.

Thought Leadership and Vision in RPA Vendor Selection
Importance of Vision Alignment
When selecting an RPA vendor, it is crucial to evaluate the company’s thought leadership and vision. Although
these aspects may seem intangible or nebulous, they play a significant role in determining long-term success. If
the vendor’s vision aligns closely with your organization’s goals and strategic direction, the results can be
highly impactful, fostering stronger collaboration and sustainable outcomes.
Ways to Assess Vendor Vision
To gain insights into a vendor’s vision, one can explore multiple communication channels maintained by the
company. Examining their Twitter page, blog posts, and YouTube channel is a good starting point. Company
conference videos, in particular, can provide a deeper understanding of how the vendor sees the future of
automation and the value they place on innovation. Through this exploration, organizations can determine
whether the vendor’s roadmap is clear, practical, and supportive of customer needs.
Challenges in Evaluating Vision
During this evaluation, it is not uncommon to find that some vendors lack clarity in their vision. In certain
cases, the vision may appear muddled or inconsistent, raising concerns about the company’s ability to deliver
long-term results. Even more concerning, some vendors may engage in overhyping their capabilities, presenting
an unrealistic image of what their platform can achieve.
Warning Signs of Hype
If an RPA vendor claims that its software can handle virtually every task, at a low cost, with minimal effort,
this should be seen as a red flag. Such promises often indicate a focus on generating public relations buzz and
driving sales rather than providing practical, reliable solutions. Buyers must remain cautious of these too-good-
to-be-true offers, as genuine RPA implementation always involves complexity and careful planning.
Reality of RPA Adoption
As emphasized by Arvind Jagannath, Director of Product Management at AI Foundry, RPA should not be
viewed as a magical solution capable of instantly handling all repetitive employee tasks. Instead, RPA
deployment requires tweaking, testing, and gradual adjustments, particularly in the early stages, before error
rates begin to diminish. Moreover, organizations must be prepared for an upfront time investment to properly
learn and get acquainted with the RPA tools. Only with this foundation can automation start to deliver
consistent and meaningful results.

Industry Expertise in RPA Vendor Selection
The Reality vs. The Claims
While many RPA vendors promote their platforms as being suitable for all industries, the reality is usually quite
different. In practice, most vendors tend to specialize or have deeper strengths in certain verticals. This means
that their tools, functionalities, and case studies are more effective in specific domains rather than being equally
strong across all industries.
Concentration in Vertical Markets
It is common to find that RPA vendors concentrate their expertise in areas such as banking, insurance,
healthcare, manufacturing, retail, or telecom. For example, one vendor may excel in compliance-heavy sectors
like finance and healthcare, while another may be more experienced in supply chain automation or customer
service processes.
Importance of Research
Because of these variations, it is crucial to do thorough research before finalizing an RPA vendor. You need to
identify whether the vendor has demonstrated success in your specific industry, whether through case studies,
testimonials, or published success metrics. This will help reduce risks and ensure that the platform is not just
theoretically capable, but practically proven in your vertical.
Why Industry Expertise Matters
Choosing a vendor with relevant industry expertise ensures faster deployment, smoother adaptation to your
processes, and less need for customizations. It also means the vendor is more likely to understand industry
regulations, compliance needs, and common operational pain points.

Security, Monitoring, and Deployment in RPA Systems
Security Risks in RPA
RPA software, while highly valuable, comes with potential security risks that cannot be ignored. Since bots
often interact with sensitive systems, databases, and applications, improper implementation can expose
organizations to threats such as unauthorized access, data leaks, or compliance violations.
Importance of IT Involvement
To minimize these risks, it is essential to bring the IT team into the RPA process at an early stage. Their
expertise in system security, access control, and compliance ensures that the RPA deployment aligns with
organizational security standards and industry regulations. Early IT involvement not only prevents problems but
also builds a more resilient and secure automation framework.
Need for Strong Monitoring
Bots, by nature, can be brittle because they depend on specific user interfaces, screen layouts, and workflows.
Even small changes in an application can cause a bot to fail. Hence, a strong monitoring mechanism is critical.
A robust RPA system must provide real-time monitoring tools to detect issues quickly, track performance, and
raise alerts when failures occur, ensuring smooth business continuity.
Deployment Considerations
Another key factor is deployment. Organizations must decide whether the RPA software will be deployed
through an on-premise setup or hosted on the cloud. An on-premise setup provides more control and may be
suitable for industries with strict compliance requirements. Cloud hosting, on the other hand, offers scalability,
flexibility, and easier maintenance, making it attractive for businesses looking for faster implementation and
lower infrastructure costs.

What Type of RPA?
Evaluating the Nature of Processes
When selecting RPA software, one of the most important considerations is the type of processes you intend to
automate. You must ask critical questions such as whether the processes require direct human intervention or
whether they can operate entirely in the background without manual involvement. These questions help
determine the appropriate type of automation that aligns with your organizational needs.
Attended vs. Unattended Automation
If the processes involve human interaction, then attended automation may be the right choice. Attended bots
work alongside humans, assisting them with tasks and improving efficiency during live operations. On the other
hand, if the processes are repetitive, rule-based, and can be executed without human involvement, then
unattended automation is more suitable. Unattended bots run independently, usually on servers or virtual
environments, and can complete tasks without user input.
Combination of Both Types
It is also quite common for organizations to require both attended and unattended automation, depending on
their workflow complexities. For example, some processes may begin with attended bots to assist employees
but later shift to unattended bots for back-end operations. Recognizing the need for both types ensures that
automation is applied flexibly and effectively.
Importance of Early Decision-Making
The key point is to make this distinction early in the RPA implementation process. Understanding whether you
need attended bots, unattended bots, or a combination of both allows you to choose the right RPA software.
This early clarity avoids mismatches between organizational needs and tool capabilities, ensuring a smoother
and more successful automation journey.

The Design
Steve Jobs’ Perspective on Design
A major factor behind Steve Jobs’ extraordinary success was his relentless obsession with design. He famously
remarked, “Design is a funny word. Some people think design means how it looks. But of course, if you dig
deeper, it’s really how it works.” This statement highlights a crucial distinction—design is not merely about
visual appeal, but fundamentally about functionality and usability.
Relevance to RPA Solutions
This principle directly applies when selecting an RPA solution. Every vendor will naturally claim that their
software is intuitive, simple to use, and almost magical in its design. However, the reality often tells a different
story. Once tested, many solutions fail to live up to their promises, reflecting the broader issue that much
enterprise software still falls short in terms of effective design.
Evaluating Workflow, UI, and UX
Therefore, it is essential to dedicate time to carefully evaluate the workflow, user interface (UI), and user
experience (UX) of the RPA tool under consideration. The design must be practical, functional, and aligned
with the actual needs of the employees who will interact with it.
Learning Curve and Skill Requirements
A critical question is whether the software is something workers can learn quickly and adopt without difficulty.
If the learning curve is steep, productivity gains may be undermined. Another important consideration is the
level of technical expertise required. Some RPA platforms may still demand coding skills, while others
emphasize no-code or low-code environments to ensure broader accessibility.
Core Consideration
Ultimately, the design of an RPA solution should go beyond surface-level claims of ease-of-use. It must
combine aesthetic simplicity with true functionality, enabling employees to integrate it seamlessly into their
workflows without unnecessary complexity.

Next-Generation Technologies in RPA
Lack of Transparency in Product Roadmaps
When dealing with RPA companies, one important reality is that they generally do not provide customers with
a detailed product roadmap. This reluctance arises from two main factors. First, there are competitive reasons,
since revealing too much about upcoming features could give rival companies an edge. Second, RPA
companies do not want to commit themselves to strict timelines or deadlines. If delays happen continually,
customers may lose faith, stop listening to promises, and eventually shift to competitors.
Focus Areas for RPA Companies
Despite the absence of transparent roadmaps, there are still certain areas that RPA companies must focus on in
order to remain relevant and provide long-term value. Perhaps the most crucial area is the integration of
Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) into RPA platforms. These technologies
significantly expand the capabilities of automation beyond simple repetitive tasks.
Current Limitations of RPA Bots
As it stands, RPA bots are best suited for processes involving highly repeatable and structured data. This
works well for rule-based tasks but limits their scope. However, in real-world enterprises, more than 80% of
enterprise content is unstructured, which poses a challenge for traditional bots.
AI and ML as Enablers of Smarter Automation
According to Bill Galusha, who leads Product Strategy for RPA and Content Intelligence at ABBYY, enterprise
digital transformation requires AI to complement bots. AI technologies make bots smarter by enabling them to
transform unstructured documents into structured content. This reduces friction in processes and allows
organizations to extract greater business value.
Use Cases for AI-Enhanced RPA
Use cases that involve customer touchpoints are prime candidates for AI-augmented RPA. Examples include
onboarding customers, filing insurance claims, processing loan or credit requests, and other scenarios where
large volumes of unstructured data exist. By incorporating AI, these processes become more seamless, accurate,
and efficient.
The Concept of Skills for Digital Workers
Another emerging idea is the concept of “skills” for bots or digital workers. A skill represents an add-on
capability that enhances the training and understanding of a bot, enabling it to handle more advanced content-
processing tasks. For instance, a bot could be given a skill for interpreting invoices, contracts, or emails.
Growing Marketplace for Digital Skills
As marketplaces for digital skills expand, enterprises will have access to hundreds of specialized skills that can
be integrated easily into existing bots. This trend will increasingly make digital bots smarter, more versatile,
and more effective at handling real-world business processes.

Center of Excellence (CoE)
Introduction to Adobe’s Challenge
In 2011, Adobe was facing a difficult situation as its growth had stalled, and the future prospects of the
company seemed uncertain. The CEO, Shantanu Narayen, recognized the urgent need for bold actions to put
the company back on track. His strategy included two major shifts: a transition of the business model toward
subscriptions and the cloud, and a stronger push to foster innovation within the organization. A notable step in
this direction was the Sneaks program, through which employees could submit new ideas, and Adobe would
allocate resources to commercialize selected ones.
Need for Automation and Efficiency
Besides subscriptions, cloud adoption, and innovation, Adobe identified another crucial pillar for
transformation: streamlining operations through automation. With more than 21,000 employees across the
globe, manual and repetitive processes were slowing down efficiency. To address this, Adobe launched its
Robotic Process Automation (RPA) implementation in 2018, although the process came with several
challenges due to the scale and complexity of the company.
Setting Up the CoE
To overcome these challenges, Adobe established a Center of Excellence (CoE) for RPA. This centralized
structure provided guidance, best practices, and governance for the company’s automation journey. In a blog
post for The Enterprisers Project, Adobe’s Senior Vice President and Chief Information Officer, Cynthia
Stoddard, highlighted the importance of this approach. She stated that Adobe was still in the growth phase of
RPA implementation and continued to discover new applications for it. Importantly, she emphasized that any
CIO or IT leadership team must insist on setting up a CoE as part of an RPA program, pointing out that
Adobe would not have achieved such significant results so quickly without one.
Initial Focus on Finance Department
The CoE first concentrated its efforts on the finance department, an area with a high volume of repetitive and
rules-based processes. The outcomes were impressive and measurable:
• 79% reduction in hours spent on creating purchase orders for hardware.
• The RPA system automated 82% of the tasks related to wire transfer requests.
• 82% reduction in manual hours dedicated to contract creation.
Results and Strategic Importance
These results demonstrated clear efficiency gains, cost savings, and productivity improvements. The RPA
initiative under the guidance of the CoE became a strategic priority for Adobe, proving that automation, when
properly managed, can transform core business functions and free employees to focus on higher-value tasks.
Transition to Best Practices
After seeing standout results, Adobe’s experience highlights how a Center of Excellence is not optional but
essential for scaling RPA. The CoE provides governance, shared expertise, and strategic alignment, enabling
large organizations like Adobe to realize the full potential of automation. This chapter therefore focuses on
examining the role of the CoE and the best practices companies should adopt when assembling an RPA team.

Center of Excellence (CoE) in RPA
A Center of Excellence (CoE) is a critical concept in ensuring the success and sustainability of Robotic Process
Automation (RPA) within an organization. It represents a formalized structure for embedding RPA deeply into
the organization’s operations and for redistributing accumulated knowledge, resources, and expertise across
future deployments.
Definition of CoE
According to UiPath, a CoE is “essentially the way to embed RPA deeply and effectively into the organization,
and to redistribute accumulated knowledge and resources across future deployments.” This means that a CoE is
not just a group of people but rather a system of governance, leadership, and guidance that ensures RPA
adoption remains scalable, structured, and effective.
Variations in CoE Structures
There is no single model for a CoE. Its structure can vary depending on the size, requirements, and complexity
of the organization. For instance:
• It could be a small team of two or three members responsible for overseeing RPA adoption.
• It could be a larger and more formal team with multiple specialists, project managers, and technical
experts.
• An organization might even establish separate CoEs for different departments or divisions, each
catering to its specific automation needs.
Focus on the “E” in CoE
The most important element of a CoE lies in the “E” which stands for Excellence. Excellence involves ensuring
that RPA is implemented with precision, focus, and maximum impact. A blog post from Automation Anywhere
emphasizes that businesses require focus and alignment for successful RPA implementation. A CoE provides
this by offering:
• Leadership to guide strategy and adoption
• Best practices to ensure consistency and standardization
• Research and innovation for new opportunities
• Focus on efficiency to improve business performance
Reasons for Implementing a CoE
There are multiple motivations behind setting up a CoE in RPA. These include:
• Ensuring compliance with regulations
• Achieving cost reduction through automation
• Improving service levels for customers and stakeholders
• Driving revenue growth through efficiency
• Minimizing errors by reducing manual intervention
A CoE helps organizations prioritize effectively. For example, while there may be 200 automation ideas in the
pipeline, the CoE ensures that focus is directed toward implementing a few high-value automation initiatives
first. This approach prevents scattered efforts and maximizes ROI.
Monitoring and Metrics through CoE
As an indication of the growing importance of CoEs, Automation Anywhere has developed its own CoE
dashboard. Accessible via a mobile app, this dashboard allows businesses to view critical metrics of their RPA
system, such as ROI, time savings, and other performance indicators. This real-time visibility ensures
accountability and helps demonstrate the tangible value of automation initiatives to stakeholders.
Ownership and Operation of CoE
A CoE can be structured in different ways in terms of ownership:
• It can be fully managed internally by the company that is implementing RPA, ensuring full control and
alignment with organizational goals.
• Alternatively, it can be outsourced to a consulting firm, especially when external expertise is required.
However, even in this case, members from the client company usually participate to maintain
collaboration and alignment with business objectives.
This flexibility allows organizations to adopt a CoE model that best suits their capabilities, resources, and
strategic goals.

Why Have a CoE (Center of Excellence) in RPA?
Drawbacks of Having a CoE
There are very few drawbacks when creating a CoE. The most reasonable limitation is that if an organization is
only testing RPA technology on a small scale, then there may not be a need to establish a formal CoE. A CoE
becomes relevant and essential when the organization is serious about long-term RPA adoption and scaling.
Commitment and Seriousness of the Organization
If a company is committed to making RPA technology work for the long haul, then establishing a CoE should
be an integral part of the process. A CoE is a clear sign that the organization is serious about automation. It
reflects a willingness to invest both time and money into the initiative. Employees also take notice of this
commitment, which helps in smoother training and adoption. This commitment is further highlighted if some or
all members of the CoE are working full-time on RPA.
Advantages of Having a CoE
1. Holistic 360-Degree View – A CoE provides a complete, end-to-end view of the RPA project. This
avoids the challenges of silos and prevents the existence of multiple RPA systems within the same
organization. As a result, implementation becomes faster, bots are created more effectively, and
monitoring becomes more thorough.
2. Cost Savings and Control – If the CoE is self-run, it can lower costs compared to outsourcing or
relying on external vendors. Beyond cost, it gives the organization more control over RPA initiatives,
creating better opportunities to experiment, innovate, and refine processes.
3. Scalability Support – Early RPA projects often see success, but scaling technology across the
enterprise is where challenges appear. A CoE helps overcome this barrier by focusing concentrated
effort on the RPA system, making scaling more structured and achievable.
4. Organizational Buy-in – The CoE encourages involvement from different levels of the organization,
including the C-suite. For example, Adobe’s RPA success was partly due to collaboration between the
CIO and CFO. Equally important is the involvement of frontline employees who use the technology
daily, ensuring adoption is effective and practical.
Expert Insights on the Role of CoE
According to Agustin Huerta, VP of Technology, AI, and Process Automation Studios at Globant,
implementing a CoE for automation is crucial to sustain long-term RPA success. He highlights that a proper
CoE serves as the guardian of technical standards and enforces a single methodology for automation. This
ensures the technology works effectively across the organization. Furthermore, the CoE acts as a gatekeeper for
deciding which processes should be automated. This ensures that only processes with proper return on
investment (ROI) are pursued. Additionally, the CoE governs the new digital workforce, ensuring structure,
consistency, and value.

Forming the Team for RPA Center of Excellence (CoE)
Timing of CoE Formation
The establishment of a Center of Excellence (CoE) generally takes place four to six months after the start of an
RPA project. This timeline is considered practical because it allows the organization to gather valuable
learnings during the early phase, such as through proof-of-concept experiments. However, even during this
initial period, it is advisable to start planning for the CoE by identifying potential members and clarifying the
goals. This preparation ensures a smoother transition once the CoE is formally launched.
Need for Broad Organizational Representation
A CoE should represent a broad spectrum of the organization that is impacted by RPA implementation.
Successful automation requires collaboration between business process experts, technology teams, and
automation professionals. This balance helps ensure that both the process management and the technical aspects
of automation are addressed effectively.
Role of External Experts and SMEs
Often, firms engage external experts to support the CoE team, particularly to provide insights into how RPA
works in practice. Subject Matter Experts (SMEs) collaborate with automation and process management teams
to identify and prioritize effective automation strategies. These SMEs bring essential domain knowledge that
complements technical automation expertise.
Involvement of the IT Team
It is critical that the technology or IT team is part of the CoE from the very beginning. Although IT
professionals may not be deeply familiar with process-based work, they are crucial in managing and supporting
the automation technology. Their involvement ensures that the platform is configured in alignment with unique
requirements such as security, compliance, and auditing. Early IT engagement also helps them gain a deeper
understanding of the RPA platform, which eventually allows IT teams to adopt digital workers for automating
their own internal processes. This collaboration helps integrate the digital workforce seamlessly into the core
operations of the organization.
UiPath’s Approach to CoE Roles
UiPath provides a detailed model for setting up a CoE, involving an extensive list of roles. However, when
starting out, it is recommended to begin with a smaller set of roles and gradually expand as the project matures.
Still, examining UiPath’s model is valuable to understand the potential scope of CoE responsibilities.
Key Roles within the CoE
• RPA Sponsor: An executive from the business side who provides overall support and sponsorship for
the RPA initiative. This role ensures alignment between RPA activities and the strategic goals of the
business.
• RPA Champion: An evangelist responsible for generating enthusiasm and excitement for the RPA
project. The champion engages employees through initiatives like videos, workshops, and blog posts to
spread awareness and foster acceptance.
• RPA Change Manager: Similar to the champion, this role focuses on securing buy-in from
stakeholders. A major responsibility includes creating and executing a communication plan to keep all
stakeholders informed and supportive of the project.
• RPA Service Support: This role serves as the first line of support for the RPA project. It involves
handling initial issues, troubleshooting, and providing assistance to ensure smooth functioning.
• RPA Infrastructure Engineer: A highly technical role responsible for server installation, monitoring,
and architecture design of the RPA implementation. This requires deep expertise in network protocols,
administration, security, cloud systems, server layouts, database technologies, and virtualization
platforms like VMware, Citrix, or Hyper-V.
Main Roles Seen in Industry Job Postings
While many roles contribute to a CoE, job postings often emphasize a few core positions as most critical. These
include:
• Business Analyst: Focused on understanding business processes and identifying automation
opportunities.
• Developer: Responsible for building and coding the RPA bots.
• Solution Architect: Designs the overall RPA solution, ensuring scalability, efficiency, and integration
with existing systems.
• RPA Supervisor: Manages and monitors the performance of bots, ensuring that they function
effectively in production environments.
This structure highlights the balance between business understanding, technical expertise, solution design, and
operational management in a successful RPA CoE.

Business Analyst in RPA Implementation
Role and Importance
The Business Analyst plays a critical role in bridging the gap between the company’s SMEs (subject matter
experts), RPA supervisors, and the developers. This position requires balancing the requirements of revenue
and cost goals with technical feasibility, making it both challenging and vital. A business analyst must possess a
combination of business knowledge, process planning skills, and technological understanding, along with a
deep familiarity with the RPA platform.
Core Responsibilities
A Business Analyst undertakes multiple responsibilities that ensure the smooth design, implementation, and
maintenance of RPA projects. These include:
1. Documentation Preparation
The analyst develops detailed documents that clearly outline the goals of the project and what needs to be
accomplished. A key deliverable is the Process Design Document (PDD), usually prepared with the help of
RPA developers, which defines the process flow and automation requirements.
2. Process Improvement Analysis
The Business Analyst actively examines the company’s existing processes and identifies areas for
improvement. This leads to the creation of an Opportunity Assessment Document, which highlights which
processes are best suited for automation and how they can enhance efficiency.
3. Technical Awareness
Although not a developer, the analyst is expected to have a high-level understanding of technologies, such as
programming languages and artificial intelligence. This knowledge helps in better collaboration with
developers and in evaluating solutions.
4. Evaluation of RPA Tools
The role also involves experience in comparing and assessing different RPA solutions, ensuring the chosen
platform aligns with the organization’s needs in terms of cost, scalability, and performance.
5. IT Collaboration
The Business Analyst works closely with IT teams to address issues of integration, security, and governance,
ensuring that RPA implementations fit seamlessly into the organization’s existing technology infrastructure.
6. Test Case Development
Another key responsibility is to design test cases to verify whether the RPA implementation meets the intended
project goals. This ensures that bots function as expected and provide the desired outcomes.
7. Contribution to CoE Formation
The Business Analyst plays a part in helping to establish the Center of Excellence (CoE), contributing
knowledge, documentation, and strategic alignment to the overall RPA framework within the organization.
8. Bot Monitoring and Maintenance
Post-deployment, the analyst assists in monitoring and maintaining bots, ensuring they continue to deliver
expected results and adapting them as business requirements evolve.
9. Additional Role as Project Manager
Depending on the size of the RPA team, the Business Analyst may also assume the role of project manager,
overseeing the entire RPA lifecycle from planning to execution and monitoring.

What Should a CoE Do?
Misconceptions About CoEs
The term “Center of Excellence” (CoE) can sometimes create confusion. To some, it may sound like just
another name for a committee, which can give the impression of inactivity or bureaucracy. As humorist Fred
Allen once said: “A committee is a group of people who individually can do nothing, but who, as a group, can
meet and decide that nothing can be done.” Unfortunately, this critique has applied to certain CoEs, where
performance has been inconsistent and the group ends up being a nonfactor. However, successful cases, such as
Adobe’s CoE, highlight that the difference lies in establishing a clear mission and executing it effectively.
Operating Models for CoE Functions
Infosys Consulting offers a practical framework for what a CoE should do, dividing it into two stages: the
automation CoE (setup phase) and the scalable automation execution engine (recurring phase).
Stage One: Automation CoE (Setup Phase)
• Automation Strategy: This involves defining the vision and roadmap of the RPA project. The CoE
must assess the business case, identify which functions to automate first, establish ROI targets, and set
qualitative goals such as innovation and customer service improvements.
• Technology Strategy: More than simply selecting an RPA platform, this includes considering
complementary technologies such as OCR, NLP, and AI. The strategy must also ensure scalability and a
solid long-term foundation for automation.
• Governance: A robust compliance framework with clearly defined roles and responsibilities must be
implemented. Governance is often overlooked, and when neglected, it can cause significant failures in
RPA execution.
• People Enablement: Beyond technical skills, successful RPA implementation requires strong project
management and change management. Training and upskilling must be addressed here.
• Operating Model: This focuses on how the RPA function is structured within the organization,
primarily on the business side.
• Bot Development and Support: Standards and methodologies must be established for automation
development. The CoE should also look into strategies for reusing bots (for example, leveraging a bot
store) and maintaining them over time.
Stage Two: Scalable Automation Execution Engine (Recurring Activities)
Once the RPA system is in place, the CoE transitions into managing ongoing execution:
• Demand Management: Identifying, prioritizing, and approving new automation opportunities.
• Bot Development: Designing, building, testing, and deploying bots into production.
• Bot Support: Continuous monitoring, exception handling, and ensuring uptime and efficiency.
• Organizational Change Management: Driving adoption across the organization by embedding RPA
into day-to-day operations, aligning culture, and overcoming resistance.
Models for Deploying a CoE
Infosys also outlines three ways of structuring a CoE within an organization:
• Centralized Model: In this approach, the CoE manages all RPA duties. It works well for highly
centralized organizations, in early implementation stages, or where automation opportunities are still
limited. Advantages include easier knowledge sharing, standardized best practices, and lower costs.
However, for larger organizations, it may become harder to manage and less flexible.
• Decentralized Model: Here, multiple CoEs operate across different departments. This is beneficial
when departments have unique requirements, allowing for greater ROI through tailored automation.
However, the risks include the creation of silos, duplication of work, and higher costs.
• Federated Model: This hybrid approach centralizes the automation CoE but decentralizes the scalable
automation execution engine across the organization. It is designed to capture the benefits of both
centralized and decentralized models, though it is complex to implement and requires careful
coordination.

COMMUNICATION IN RPA IMPLEMENTATION AND CoE CONTEXT
Fear and Misunderstanding Around Automation
When RPA is introduced into an organization, the terms “automation” and “AI” often trigger fear and
uncertainty among employees. Workers may worry about job losses or suspect that they are essentially training
bots to replace themselves. This kind of anxiety is natural and if not managed properly, it can result in
resistance, low morale, and even open opposition to the RPA initiative.
Lessons from The Office Example
The comedy show The Office demonstrates a good analogy here. In the first season, employees feared
downsizing, but their boss, Michael Scott, avoided discussing the issue. Instead, he made a poor comparison to
a doctor not telling a patient they had cancer. While exaggerated for humor, the message highlights a common
real-life leadership mistake: avoiding sensitive topics only makes things worse. Similarly, avoiding
conversations about RPA’s impact will only heighten rumors, insecurities, and mistrust among employees.
The Imperative of Transparency
Transparency must be a core principle in communication during RPA adoption. Employees can usually sense
when they are being misled or when information is hidden. Therefore, leaders should:
• Share the goals of the RPA project openly with all impacted employees.
• Clearly disclose if one of the objectives involves reducing headcount. While uncomfortable, honesty
builds credibility and prevents distrust.
• Balance this by also communicating the benefits of RPA, such as reducing tedious manual tasks, giving
employees more time to focus on valuable and strategic work, and enabling higher productivity.
Repetition and Reinforcement of Messaging
Communication cannot be a one-time announcement. The messaging about RPA’s purpose and benefits should
be repeated consistently across multiple channels. Leaders can use:
• Videos, blogs, and educational materials to explain the project.
• Guest speakers from companies that have successfully implemented RPA, or short testimonial videos
from such companies, to provide real-world success examples.
Balancing Enthusiasm with Realism
While it is important to highlight the benefits, communication must also remain realistic. Overpromising will
only lead to disappointment when expectations are not met. The safe approach is to follow the old adage:
“Underpromise and overdeliver.” This way, employees are more likely to view the project positively as it
progresses.
Accessibility of Management
Management must remain approachable and open to questions at all times. Employees should feel they can
raise concerns, seek clarifications, and get honest responses. This accessibility reinforces trust and confidence
in leadership. As Meg Whitman, former CEO of Hewlett-Packard and eBay, once stated: “Stop the emails and
start talking to your teams. Just letting people know that you’re conscious of the challenges, aware of the issues
and actively dealing with them matters.” This emphasizes that improving communication is not about tools or
corporate initiatives alone—it depends on individual leadership action and continuous dialogue.
Continuous Communication as a Process
Ultimately, communication in the context of RPA and the CoE is not a one-time activity but a continuous
process. It requires transparency, accessibility, repeated reinforcement, balanced optimism, and the personal
involvement of leadership to truly manage the fears, expectations, and engagement of employees.

CHANGE MANAGEMENT IN THE CONTEXT OF RPA
Case Example of J. C. Penney and Lessons Learned
In June 2011, J. C. Penney appointed Ron Johnson, a highly acclaimed executive from Apple and Target, as
CEO. Initially, investor optimism sent the stock soaring, and Johnson himself invested $50 million in company
stock. He promised to reimagine the department store model, but his radical redesigns, pricing overhauls, and
shift to younger demographics alienated the loyal customer base. Sales plummeted, employees became
demoralized, and productivity dropped. This case illustrates a classic failure of change management—too
much change, implemented too quickly, without accounting for cultural and customer realities. The lesson is
clear: people do not easily accept radical transformation, and without careful change management, even
brilliant strategies can collapse.
Importance of Change Management in RPA
Just as in retail, RPA implementations face resistance. Employees often fear losing jobs, being replaced by bots,
or being forced to move to different roles. Additionally, many organizations run on legacy systems, sometimes
even mainframes, which creates technical and cultural friction. Without strong change management, RPA
projects risk failure. In fact, inadequate change management is often one of the leading causes of RPA
implementation breakdowns.
John Kotter’s Eight-Step Process for Change Management
Management consultant John Kotter’s framework, based on research from over 100 organizations, provides a
structured eight-step approach to leading change:
Step 1 – Create a Sense of Urgency
Change requires momentum, and this begins with urgency. Leaders must present a compelling story, analogy,
or case to motivate action, avoiding jargon and excessive data. The goal is to win both hearts and minds. A
strong example is Mark Zuckerberg in 2012, who personally led Facebook’s pivot to mobile, demonstrating
urgency and commitment by living on his phone and stressing the existential nature of the transition.
Step 2 – Build a Guiding Coalition
Change must be powered by influential figures across the organization—not only executives but also those with
credibility, expertise, leadership skills, and strong reputations. This coalition creates momentum that legitimizes
the project.
Step 3 – Form a Change Vision
Every transformation needs a destination. The vision should be clear, realistic, attainable, flexible, and
inspiring. Kotter suggests a litmus test: if the vision cannot be explained in under five minutes in a way that
sparks understanding and interest, it is not yet effective.
Step 4 – Communicating the Vision
A common pitfall is to create a vision and then under-communicate it. Great leaders repeat the vision
constantly—sometimes to the point it feels excessive. Kotter recommends amplifying the message by a factor
of ten, since employees absorb new ideas slowly. Patience and persistence are critical.
Step 5 – Empower Broad-Based Action
To drive change, leaders must remove barriers and recognize employee efforts, even when outcomes fall short.
Experimentation must be encouraged, and failures accepted, as long as the vision remains the guiding force.
Obstacles can include compensation structures, rigid titles, or psychological barriers. Leaders must actively
dismantle these blockers.
Step 6 – Generate Short-Term Wins
Large-scale transformation takes years, and waiting too long for visible success risks demoralizing the
organization. Short-term wins keep momentum alive, build credibility, and energize the workforce to stay
committed.
Step 7 – Consolidate Gains and Produce More Change
Short-term wins should not signal completion. Leaders must frame them as milestones in a longer journey,
reinforcing long-term goals and pushing forward to avoid stagnation.
Step 8 – Anchor New Approaches in the Culture
Sustaining change is the most difficult challenge. The goal is to embed change into organizational culture so
that employees naturally say, “This is how we do things here.” To achieve this, communication must highlight
clear examples of improvements from the change, and sufficient time must be given for new managers and
employees to adopt the approach.
Kotter’s Model in Practice
While Kotter’s process is rigorous and proven, it is also difficult to execute. It demands leadership persistence,
patience, and deep cultural alignment. Nevertheless, organizations that follow it often achieve lasting success.
Additional Considerations for RPA Change Management
Gauge the Sentiment
Leaders must actively measure whether employees are embracing change. Tools such as periodic surveys (e.g.,
via SurveyMonkey) can provide insights into organizational sentiment and highlight areas of resistance that
need attention.
Gamification
To make RPA adoption engaging, organizations can introduce game-like elements. For example, awarding
scores to projects, creating contests, or offering prizes for the most creative bots. Gamification transforms the
adoption journey into an interactive and motivating experience.
Strategy Alignment
Even the best change management frameworks will fail if the underlying strategy is flawed. The J. C. Penney
case reinforces this point: change itself is not enough—strategic direction matters. Before implementing RPA,
organizations must carefully analyze whether automation aligns with their goals, readiness, and culture.
Rushing into RPA because it is trendy can be disastrous. Even with the right strategy, leaders must resist the
temptation to move too fast, instead allowing adequate time to educate employees and build trust in the
transition.

• A Digital Worker account is provisioned in HR and Identity Management Systems, enabling bots to
request credentials programmatically.
• Once bots are created and provisioned into Intuit Financial Systems, their credentials are stored
securely and updated after every login.
• When a process is no longer needed, the bot is de-provisioned and reassigned with a new set of
credentials, following the same process.
• This system leverages existing human access management controls, ensuring consistency, security,
and accountability.
