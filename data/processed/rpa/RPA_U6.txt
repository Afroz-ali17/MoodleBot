Open Source RPA & Future of RPA
What Is Open Source Software?
Introduction:
Open Source Software refers to software whose source code is made freely available for anyone to use, modify, and
distribute. This concept promotes collaboration, transparency, and innovation in software development.
Origin and Background:
The idea of open source software can be traced back to the 1970s and 1980s, when programmers in universities and
research labs shared code freely. One of the key figures behind this movement was Richard Stallman, who worked at
the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory.
Richard Stallman and the Free Software Movement:
Richard Stallman became concerned about the restrictions copyrights imposed on programmers. In response, he
launched the Free Software Movement in the early 1980s. His goal was to create a completely free operating system
similar to Unix, which was then controlled by AT&T. He named it GNU, which stands for “GNU’s Not Unix.”
Philosophy Behind Free Software:
Stallman’s approach was not just about giving software away for free. It was about granting freedom to use, study,
modify, and share the software. Anyone could improve or sell the software as long as they allowed others the same
rights to use and modify it. This collaborative approach led to faster innovation than traditional software development.
Rise of Linux and Acceleration of Open Source:
In the early 1990s, Linus Torvalds, a Finnish programmer, developed the Linux kernel, which was missing in GNU.
The combination of GNU tools and the Linux kernel created a complete operating system known as GNU/Linux.
Linux became widely used, especially for web servers, and inspired the growth of companies like Red Hat.
Transition from “Free Software” to “Open Source Software:”
By the late 1990s, the term “free software” created confusion for businesses, as it sounded like “no cost.” To make it
more acceptable in the corporate world, developers introduced the term “Open Source Software” in 1998. The new
term emphasized open collaboration and transparency rather than just being “free.”
The Business Model of Open Source
Introduction:
The concept of open source software may seem counterintuitive because the software is made freely available to
everyone. However, many companies have successfully built profitable businesses using open source models. These
companies generate significant revenues and even reach billion-dollar valuations by strategically combining free and
paid components.
Different Use Cases of Open Source:
Open source software can be applied in different business contexts. Some companies use it to expand market reach,
while others use it to build enterprise-level software platforms. The main idea is that by providing free access to the
software, companies can increase adoption, build a large user base, and later monetize it through additional services,
features, or platforms.
Example 1: Google and Android
When Google developed its mobile operating system, it adopted the open source model and created Android. Initially,
analysts questioned how a free operating system could generate profit. However, Google realized that making Android
freely available would encourage widespread use among smartphone manufacturers such as Samsung, creating a
massive global user base.
This large user base allowed Google to earn substantial revenue through:
1. Advertising income generated from the millions of Android devices connected to Google services.
2. Fees from the Google Play Store, where app developers pay to distribute applications and Google
earns commissions on sales.
According to a lawsuit from Oracle, as of 2015, Android had generated $31 billion in revenues and
$22 billion in profits, proving that open source software could be highly profitable when tied to a
strong ecosystem.
Example 2: Elastic – Enterprise Software through Open Source
Another successful open source business model is Elastic, founded by Shay Banon. In 2004, Banon initially created a
search engine for recipes called Compass, which later evolved into Elasticsearch, an open source search and analytics
engine. The software gained massive popularity due to its open availability and usefulness across many applications.
In 2012, Banon collaborated with Steven Schuurman, Uri Boness, and Simon Willnauer to establish Elastic, the
company behind Elasticsearch. They later expanded their product ecosystem with:
• Logstash: A data ingestion tool for collecting and sending logs.
• Kibana: A visualization tool for representing search and analytics results.
These tools collectively formed the Elastic Stack (ELK Stack), which became widely adopted in the
software industry.
Elastic’s Monetization Strategy:
Elastic follows a hybrid business model, combining open source and proprietary software.
• The open source version attracts a large number of users and organizations.
• The proprietary features and enterprise versions generate revenue from businesses that require
advanced capabilities.
• Additional income comes from support services and cloud hosting solutions.
This strategy balances community growth with sustainable commercial success. Elastic’s approach
proved highly effective — by October 2018, when Elastic went public, it recorded $89.7 million in
revenue for the fiscal first quarter of 2020, showing a 58% year-over-year increase and achieving a
market capitalization of about $6.3 billion.
Key Insights of the Open Source Business Model:
1. Free software drives widespread adoption.
2. Revenue is generated through complementary services such as advertising, hosting, support, or
premium features.
3. Community contribution and transparency enhance software quality and innovation.
4. Hybrid strategies combining free and paid components ensure sustainability.
The Pros and Cons of Open Source Software
Introduction:
Open source software offers both significant advantages and notable drawbacks. It allows users to freely access,
modify, and distribute software, promoting flexibility and innovation. However, it also poses challenges in control,
usability, and long-term reliability.
Advantages of Open Source Software:
1. Free Availability: The biggest advantage of open source software is that it is free. Companies can
test and evaluate it without financial risk to see if it suits their requirements.
2. Innovation: Open source projects often have active developer communities that continuously
improve the software. In many cases, open source versions surpass commercial alternatives in
performance and features.
3. Customization: Since the source code is accessible, organizations can modify the software to meet
their specific needs, leading to more effective and tailored implementations.
Disadvantages of Open Source Software:
1. Control Issues: Open source software evolves rapidly, which can make it difficult for enterprises
that prefer a clear roadmap and predictable updates. To manage this, some companies like Elastic
maintain governance structures to control final changes in the code base.
2. Code Base Confusion: Some open source projects maintain two separate code bases—one for
community developers and another for the company. This can create confusion, divergence, and
inconsistent support for customers.
3. User Interface (UI) Challenges: Developers usually focus on technology rather than design, so
open source software may lack a user-friendly interface. For instance, while Linux is powerful and
efficient, it has limited adoption among general consumers compared to Windows or macOS, which
emphasize design and usability.
4. Durability Concerns: Open source projects often start with strong enthusiasm but may lose
momentum over time as newer alternatives appear. This uncertainty poses a risk for corporate users
who depend on long-term stability and community support.
5. Skill Requirements: Implementing and maintaining open source software requires technical
expertise for setup, configuration, hosting, and ongoing maintenance. Hence, organizations need
skilled IT staff.
6. Lack of Vendor Backing: Unlike commercial vendors, open source projects rarely offer warranties,
service guarantees, or legal protection. This absence of formal support can be a major concern for
enterprise customers.
OpenRPA
OpenRPA is an open-source Robotic Process Automation (RPA) platform developed by Allan Zimmermann. During
the late 1990s, Zimmermann worked as a systems architect in the ISP department at Denmark’s largest telco, TDC,
where he helped deploy technologies like Exchange, NetApp, IIS, Streaming, and Lotus Notes for automation and
provisioning. Later, he gained experience in developing provisioning systems and providing high-end consulting
services.
In 2017, Zimmermann started receiving requests related to RPA projects. To explore this field, he began by testing the
Kryon Systems solution. At that time, he was already developing a workflow engine that combined human workflows
with IT and IoT. When major RPA vendors showed interest only in implementation partnerships, he decided to create
his own RPA engine, which became OpenRPA.
OpenRPA includes all the core functions expected from an RPA system, such as a designer to create bots, a debugger,
and orchestration capabilities. It is built on open-source technologies like MongoDB and NodeRED, which support
the handling of large volumes of data. This makes OpenRPA particularly effective for applications in IoT (Internet of
Things) environments.
A key feature of OpenRPA is its ability to save projects locally, which is beneficial for systems not connected to the
Internet or those requiring high levels of privacy. The platform supports auditing and traceability, and provides on-
the-fly AES 265-bit encryption, enabling compliance with international data protection standards such as GDPR
(General Data Protection Regulation) and HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act).
OpenRPA also offers integration with over 2,000 IT systems, facilitating better communication between bots. It
supports on-premise deployment as well as deployment on various cloud platforms, including Azure, AWS, IBM,
and Alibaba. According to Zimmermann, the major distinction of OpenRPA is its approach to non-RPA integration
in the backend, unlike other RPA tools that rely on additional plugins or add-ons.
When Zimmermann began this project, there were no open-source RPA solutions based on a workflow designer, and
only one existing tool had a backend, which was merely a scheduling engine. OpenRPA was therefore developed as a
comprehensive platform capable of automating every aspect of a business process, covering IT systems, human
tasks, and connected devices. By integrating RPA, workflows, AI, and IoT into one secure and open platform,
OpenRPA aims to enhance automation efficiency and create a positive impact on businesses and people’s lives.
UI.Vision
In 2001, Ann Do and Mathias Roth founded iOpus, a company that developed an application called iMacros, which
was a macro recorder tool for web browser forms. The tool gained massive popularity with over one million active
daily users. The founders later sold iOpus in 2012 to Ipswitch. In 2016, Do and Roth launched a new start-up named
a9t9, which led to the creation of UI.Vision, a platform designed to simplify automation using visual methods.
According to Mathias Roth, the idea for visual RPA came from the growing complexity and frustration of dealing with
various browser internals such as Internet Explorer, Firefox, and Chrome while developing iMacros. The goal was to
create a system that allowed users to automate their daily tasks easily without getting involved in the technical
complexities of browsers.
UI.Vision is primarily based on the latest innovations in computer vision. It follows a visual approach to automation,
operating in the same way a human does—by observing the screen visually. This allows UI.Vision to work with any
kind of application, including web and desktop applications, across multiple operating systems like Mac, Linux,
and Windows. Roth described this visual method as “the most powerful approach to automation.”
For non-visual tasks, Roth recommends using established scripting languages such as PowerShell or Python, rather
than creating a new scripting system. UI.Vision provides a command-line API that allows seamless integration with
these scripting languages. This means users can call UI.Vision from within a script and the RPA software can return
data back to the scripting environment, allowing for flexible automation workflows.
UI.Vision supports a wide range of use cases, including Citrix automations, which are often challenging, and
automation via remote access tools like TeamViewer, AnyDesk, or RDP. It is also suitable for automating modern
and complex web applications, such as Gmail and Facebook, which are difficult to handle using traditional
Selenium-style automation due to their complex structures and dynamic elements.
In terms of the business model, UI.Vision RPA software is available for free as UI.Vision RPA Core, while the
company a9t9 offers paid add-ons under the UI.Vision RPA XModules system that provide additional premium
features. The platform is designed with a strong focus on security. Roth emphasized that all Internet access is
restricted to the open-source core, while the closed-source add-on packages operate locally and never send data
externally or “call home.” This design makes UI.Vision a secure and reliable RPA solution for users across different
environments and industries.
Robocorp
Antti Karjalainen, the founder of Robocorp, possesses a strong background well-suited for the RPA (Robotic Process
Automation) industry. He holds two master’s degrees in Information/Service Management and Automation
Technology/Industrial Management. After completing his studies, he worked in software automation and led the
RPA segment of the Robot Framework open-source initiative. His experiences convinced him that the open-source
model was the best approach for developing scalable automation solutions.
In January 2019, Karjalainen established Robocorp in Finland to build a comprehensive RPA platform from the
ground up. He initially worked from his garage, demonstrating the startup’s humble beginnings. However, to achieve
global scalability, he recognized the need for significant financial backing. According to Karjalainen, “Funding is
critical to create a global ecosystem. Our vision was to make something like GitHub for automation. Instead of sharing
code, Robocorp would allow for the sharing of processes.”
To bring this vision to life, Karjalainen first secured $500,000 in angel funding. Later, in November 2019, Robocorp
raised $5.6 million in a venture capital round led by Benchmark, with participation from Slow Ventures, firstminute
Capital, Bret Taylor (President and Chief Product Officer of Salesforce), and Rob Bearden (CEO of Docker).
Benchmark partner Peter Fenton, a notable Silicon Valley investor behind companies like Elastic, Twitter, and Yelp,
joined Robocorp’s board of directors, further strengthening its leadership and credibility.
Robocorp focuses on a developer-centric approach, making open source its foundation for fostering innovation and
accessibility. Karjalainen explained that traditional RPA systems are often expensive, where a bot must typically
replace about half a year’s worth of an employee’s hours to be cost-effective. In contrast, Robocorp’s open-source
model aims to make automation affordable and widely adoptable, introducing the concept of “robosourcing”—the
idea of using robots to perform business tasks efficiently across organizations of all sizes.
The core of the Robocorp framework is built on the Robot Framework, an established open-source automation
platform. In addition, Robocorp is developing a cloud platform to manage orchestration, security, and access
control. These capabilities are designed to be offered under a Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) model, making
deployment and management more accessible for businesses.
In June 2019, Robocorp launched a private beta program involving ten companies across diverse industries,
including consulting, integration, finance, and logistics. The goal was to refine the software and study its impact on
real-world operations. The results were highly encouraging — one banking client reported saving 1,369 years of
customer waiting time within a single month of implementing Robocorp’s automation solutions.
Robocorp planned to make its software publicly available in the first half of 2020, aiming to revolutionize RPA
through open source, cloud integration, and a developer-first ecosystem that democratizes automation for businesses
worldwide.
Orchestra
Adam Marcus, a computer scientist and entrepreneur, strongly believes in combining human intelligence with the
power of computers and software. His primary focus was on how human insight could be effectively embedded within
automated systems. During his time at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), in the Computer Science
and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (CSAIL), he explored this idea extensively for his dissertation. His research
later culminated in a book titled “Crowdsourced Data Management: Industry and Academic Perspectives.”
Marcus was not just an academic; he was also a practical innovator. To apply his ideas, he co-founded a start-up called
Locu, which utilized machine learning and human participation to collect and manage data about restaurant
pricing. The platform automated the process of determining what every restaurant charges for food, combining
artificial intelligence with crowdsourced human input for accuracy and efficiency. The success of this hybrid approach
led to Locu being acquired by GoDaddy for $70 million in 2013.
Building on this experience, Marcus realized the potential for creating automation software that could enhance worker
productivity by integrating humans directly into the automation loop. This concept became the foundation for
Orchestra, which he launched in 2015. Orchestra is an open-source platform designed to enable seamless
collaboration between humans and machines through structured workflows and intelligent process management.
The platform includes several key components and features that enhance its effectiveness:
1. Workflows:
This is the core component of Orchestra. It provides features for reviewing tasks, managing
projects, and distributing work between human users and automated systems. Workflows ensure
that both people and machines contribute efficiently to completing complex processes.
2. StaffBot:
StaffBot is designed to streamline the staffing process. It automates the assignment of team
members to specific projects or tasks quickly and effectively, eliminating unnecessary manual
coordination and administrative steps.
3. SanityBot:
SanityBot acts as a project monitoring tool, ensuring that all workflows are on track. It operates
through Slack channels, automatically checking progress updates and helping teams maintain
consistency and accountability throughout a project’s lifecycle.
4. Time Tracking:
Orchestra includes an in-built time tracking system that estimates the duration required for each
task or project. This helps in analyzing productivity, improving project planning, and optimizing the
allocation of resources.
By combining automation with human oversight and collaboration, Orchestra represents a new generation of open-
source platforms aimed at enhancing workplace efficiency. It allows organizations to integrate human intelligence,
machine learning, and process automation in a single framework, making it a significant contribution to modern
workflow management and intelligent automation.
Future of RPA: Consolidation and IPOs
The Robotic Process Automation (RPA) market is witnessing rapid growth with more than 70 vendors currently
competing in the industry. Due to the high interest from venture capitalists and the increasing number of players, the
market is expected to experience significant consolidation in the coming years. Many RPA companies will find it
challenging to differentiate themselves and attract potential customers, leading to mergers and acquisitions.
Major RPA players like UiPath, Automation Anywhere, and Blue Prism have already started acquiring smaller
companies, and this trend is expected to accelerate. The purpose of these acquisitions is to expand the RPA
technology stack, enter new industries, and increase their customer base. According to Dr. Gero Decker, CEO of
Signavio, RPA vendors must diversify beyond their core products and niche technologies to strengthen their position in
the market. He emphasizes that automation vendors should focus on broadening the scope of automation use cases,
enhancing ease of implementation, and automating beyond redundant tasks to maintain competitiveness.
Another major trend in the future of RPA will be the Initial Public Offerings (IPOs) of major RPA vendors.
Currently, Blue Prism is the only pure-play public RPA company. However, in the coming years, Automation
Anywhere and UiPath are expected to go public. The objective behind IPOs is not just to raise capital—since RPA
companies already have strong funding—but to gain prestige, liquidity, and transparency.
Prestige is achieved as only companies with strong revenues, a large customer base, and a clear path to profitability
can become public. It also increases media and analyst attention, enhancing the company’s reputation. Liquidity is
another advantage, as employees who accepted equity in place of higher salaries can convert their shares into cash
once the company is listed. Transparency plays a crucial role because public companies are legally required to
disclose key financial and operational information, enabling better industry evaluation and building trust with
customers and investors.
Hence, the future of RPA will likely be shaped by market consolidation through mergers and acquisitions, along
with public offerings by leading vendors, both of which will contribute to the maturity, stability, and credibility of
the RPA industry.
Microsoft
Historically, Microsoft has often been a follower in adopting new technologies. This was evident with its entry into
graphical user interface (GUI)-based operating systems and Internet browsers. Initially, its versions took several
iterations to become competitive, but once perfected, as seen with Windows 3.0, the results were remarkable and
helped the company dominate the market.
Despite being a late entrant in some areas, Microsoft has always possessed several strong advantages such as immense
financial resources, a trusted global brand, a vast ecosystem of partners, and a massive customer base. These
strengths have consistently enabled it to catch up quickly and establish leadership positions in emerging technologies.
Regarding Robotic Process Automation (RPA), Microsoft has now made it a strategic priority. At its Ignite
Conference in November 2019, Microsoft officially announced its entry into the RPA market. It built its RPA solution
by leveraging its existing Power Platform, which already supported Business Intelligence (BI), low-code
development, and workflow management. This platform was enhanced to create Power Automate, Microsoft’s RPA
product.
Power Automate integrates with Selenium, an open-source tool used to record and automate web-based applications.
The platform includes over 275 prebuilt connectors to various apps and services, enabling easy integration across
systems. It also features Artificial Intelligence (AI) capabilities and provides native integration with other
Microsoft products such as Office 365, Dynamics 365, and Azure.
Although some coding knowledge is required to use Power Automate, it mainly operates through a high-level, drag-
and-drop interface, making it accessible to non-technical users. Additionally, Microsoft has ensured that automation
workflows can be connected seamlessly with Microsoft Teams, allowing users to create alerts and triggers directly
within the collaboration platform.
Power Automate is considered a strong and promising RPA solution, but it currently lacks certain enterprise-grade
features such as comprehensive identity management. However, these capabilities are expected to be integrated in
the coming years as Microsoft continues to refine the product. Given Microsoft’s vast technical expertise, financial
capacity, and innovation-driven culture, it is well positioned to advance rapidly in the RPA domain and become a
key competitor in the automation industry.
Attended Automation
RPA technology has primarily focused on unattended automation, where bots operate independently without human
intervention. This approach has been easier to implement since it removes the complexity of human involvement.
However, attended automation is now emerging as a powerful approach that combines human intelligence with the
efficiency of bots to achieve better results.
In attended automation, an employee works alongside a bot, using the bot’s speed, accuracy, and intelligence while
applying their own skills and judgment to solve complex problems. This collaboration creates a strong synergy
between humans and machines, enhancing productivity and improving decision-making.
Recent advancements in integration and Artificial Intelligence (AI) have significantly improved attended
automation technology. According to Francis Carden, Vice President of Digital Automation and Robotics at PEGA,
businesses are beginning to focus more on attended automation. He emphasizes that combining bots and humans can
produce optimal outcomes quickly and effectively. In industries like call centers and large front or back offices,
attended RPA can be deployed rapidly, delivering faster return on investment (ROI) and enabling employees to
handle routine tasks efficiently.
Similarly, Barry Cooper, Enterprise Group President at NICE, highlights that organizations are realizing the value of
attended automation in improving employee performance and workload management. By providing each
employee with a personal robotic assistant, attended bots can guide them in real time, automate repetitive tasks, and
allow employees to focus on higher-value work that requires creativity and problem-solving. This not only increases
efficiency but also improves employee satisfaction and customer service quality.
A strong example of this trend is Automation Anywhere’s acquisition of Klevops, a Paris-based start-up. The
company introduced Attended Automation 2.0, which enables managers to orchestrate workflows between teams of
employees and bots. This approach enhances employee productivity and provides a better customer experience.
Moreover, it ensures centralized governance, security, and analytics, maintaining the high standards Automation
Anywhere is known for.
Attended automation is expected to gain significant traction, especially in industries dependent on contact centers,
such as banking and telecommunications, where human interaction is vital but can be augmented through
automation for improved efficiency and service delivery.
Vertical-Specific Companies
Most RPA vendors design solutions that can be used across multiple industries, but many of them still specialize in
certain domains. As RPA technology matures, there is an increasing trend toward vertical-specific companies, which
focus on developing automation solutions tailored to the needs of a particular industry. This approach allows for more
specialized and effective automation, as each industry has unique use cases and challenges that require customized
solutions.
A prime example of this trend is Olive, a company focused specifically on the healthcare industry. The founder and
CEO, Sean Lane, has a unique background as an intelligence officer in the U.S. Air Force, where he worked at the
National Security Agency (NSA) and gained valuable experience in handling large-scale engineering challenges.
After completing five tours of duty in Afghanistan and Iraq, he returned to the U.S. and became deeply concerned
about the opioid addiction crisis. Determined to make a difference, he applied his expertise in data analysis to address
inefficiencies in the healthcare sector.
Upon studying the healthcare system, Lane discovered that enterprise systems were highly proprietary, meaning they
couldn’t easily communicate with each other. These systems were also complex, unintuitive, and often required hiring
consultants or outsourcing firms to add new functionalities. This made it difficult to implement modern technologies
like Artificial Intelligence (AI).
Seeing this gap, Lane founded Olive with the goal of creating an AI-powered digital workforce. Instead of replacing
existing software systems, Olive automates repetitive and error-prone workflows, mimicking the manual tasks
performed by human employees—only much faster and more accurately. Lane’s vision is to reduce healthcare
costs by a trillion dollars while enhancing the human experience.
The name “Olive” represents a digital employee who works confidentially and efficiently. Olive assists with tasks
such as collections, coding, and credential management, and understands the complex language of healthcare,
including Electronic Health Records (EHRs), patient accounting systems, and third-party portals.
Olive is designed to be proactive and adaptive. For example, if a system error occurs, Olive automatically fixes it. If
a payer portal changes, she updates her workflow accordingly. She can even analyze data to discover cost-saving
opportunities and notify her manager. Each Olive is connected within a networked digital workforce, allowing
hospitals to learn collectively and avoid solving the same problems repeatedly. Lane refers to this as creating a “true
internet of healthcare.”
The healthcare industry is an ideal target for RPA, as nearly $1 trillion in U.S. healthcare spending is administrative.
Employees spend significant time on mundane, repetitive tasks, such as data entry and document management,
which leads to burnout, boredom, and high turnover rates. Lane envisions that even eliminating 1% of this
administrative burden could fund more research, new treatments, and medical breakthroughs, demonstrating the
transformative potential of vertical-specific RPA solutions like Olive.
Hype Factor
In 1995, Gartner analyst Jackie Fenn introduced the concept of the “Hype Cycle,” a framework that explains how
emerging technologies evolve through predictable stages of public attention and maturity. The model consists of five
main phases that illustrate the journey of a technology from innovation to mainstream adoption.
The first phase is the Technology Trigger, where a new and transformative technology emerges and captures the
attention of the media, entrepreneurs, and venture capitalists. At this stage, there are usually a few prototypes or proof-
of-concept models, but the technology’s commercial viability is still uncertain.
The second phase is the Peak of Inflated Expectations, where the technology gains momentum and public
excitement reaches its highest point. Social media and large investments accelerate this enthusiasm, often leading to
unrealistic claims and phrases like “game changer” and “inflection point.”
The third phase is the Trough of Disillusionment, where excitement fades as some companies fail or struggle to meet
expectations. Investors lose confidence, stock prices fall, and customers realize that the new technology is not
delivering the promised ROI.
The fourth phase is the Slope of Enlightenment, where the technology becomes more refined and its applications
better understood. Although the media attention decreases, the technology’s practical impact increases, and more
sustainable progress begins to take shape.
The fifth and final phase is the Plateau of Productivity, where the technology becomes mainstream, standards are
established, and its use becomes a normal part of business operations.
Although the Hype Cycle is not a perfect framework and Gartner itself has made incorrect predictions in the past, it
remains a valuable tool for understanding technological trends. It helps companies adopt a long-term perspective
and avoid investing too early in technologies with uncertain commercial value. According to Gartner, if too many
questions remain about a technology’s viability, it is better to wait until tangible value has been demonstrated.
When it comes to Robotic Process Automation (RPA), determining its exact position on the Hype Cycle is
challenging. RPA has existed for over two decades, but its rapid acceleration began only in the last six or seven years.
Based on current trends, RPA appears to be beyond the Peak of Inflated Expectations and entering the early phase
of the Trough of Disillusionment.
There is still strong media attention and abundant funding, but also increasing recognition of RPA’s challenges—
particularly in scaling implementations and integrating with AI technologies. Some major companies are
experiencing growing pains, as seen when UiPath laid off around 300 to 400 employees, or about 11% of its
workforce, in late October.
This does not indicate that UiPath or the RPA industry is in trouble. UiPath remains strong in its product offerings,
customer base, and financial health. However, the focus in the industry is shifting from rapid expansion to
sustainable growth and profitability. Following the downfall of companies like WeWork, investors and executives
are now emphasizing efficiency and balanced growth. As UiPath CEO Daniel Dines noted, organizations must
“balance growth with efficiency” to ensure long-term stability and success.
Artificial Intelligence (AI)
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is considered one of the most important drivers for Robotic Process Automation
(RPA). According to research by PwC, AI is expected to contribute about $15.7 trillion to global GDP by
2030, which exceeds the combined output of China and India. The report states that “AI touches almost
every aspect of our lives, and it’s only just getting started.”
Despite its potential, AI today remains limited and narrow. For example, AI assistants like Alexa or Siri can
handle simple commands but are still far from engaging in full, free-form conversations. Therefore, when
combining AI and RPA, businesses should focus on clear-cut and practical use cases. PwC’s report
suggests focusing on solving “boring” problems—functions like finance, HR, tax, and compliance—rather
than large-scale implementations. In 2020, only 4% of companies planned to scale AI across their
organizations, while 44% of executives saw AI as a way to improve efficiency and 42% viewed it as a
tool for productivity.
Before initiating new AI projects or purchasing new software, companies should evaluate their existing
systems, as many enterprise platforms like Oracle, Salesforce, and Microsoft already include built-in AI
capabilities. Testing these features can enhance automation and help organizations understand how to
integrate AI effectively.
Many RPA vendors are also incorporating AI features, often referred to as Intelligent Automation (IA).
However, businesses should take time to operationalize AI within their workflows, identifying which types
of AI are relevant and manageable. According to Ryan Duguid, Chief Evangelist at Nintex, “AI, especially
machine learning, has a big role to play in making sense of all the data that will be generated,” since tracking
every click and keystroke produces massive volumes of data.
Several interesting use cases combine AI and RPA effectively:
1. Corporate E-mail Inboxes: RPA can detect new emails with attachments and route them to AI tools
that use OCR and NLP to extract key information such as invoice numbers, payment terms, and
contractual details, which can then be sent to CRM or ERP systems.
2. Contract Management: RPA automates routine contract tasks, while AI uses NLP to understand
and normalize varied legal language, enabling automatic alerts for renewals or credits.
3. Invoice Automation: AI applies NLP and machine learning to extract and structure data from
differently formatted invoices, allowing RPA to handle input, validation, and reconciliation.
4. Financial Document Analysis: RPA collects data from structured sources, while AI tools with OCR
and NLP extract and convert data from unstructured PDFs into structured formats for further
automation.
5. Insurance Claims: AI can process unstructured data like images or medical scans, while RPA
automates structured claim data entry and validation.
Finally, with technologies such as Natural Language Generation (NLG), RPA and AI can automatically
produce written reports from structured datasets. According to Sharon Daniels, CEO of Arria, integrating
NLG enables instant creation of expert-level reports in natural language, extending the reach and impact of
automation in organizations.
Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) and Open Source
RPA software is currently deployed mostly on-premise, but a significant transition toward a Software-as-a-
Service (SaaS) model is expected in the near future. A cloud-native platform will make upgrades,
maintenance, and data access much easier, which will be especially critical for the integration of Artificial
Intelligence (AI) applications.
According to Antti Karjalainen, founder and CEO of Robocorp, “When the software robot becomes a
commodity, we will start to see the next wave of adoption in RPA.” He explained that small and medium-
sized businesses (SMBs) will increasingly adopt business process automation but may lack the technical
expertise to implement it independently. This will create opportunities for a new kind of service provider
called Robotics-as-a-Service (RaaS) operators. These operators can help SMEs by automating their
business routines and maintaining their software robots. RaaS providers may specialize in certain industries,
such as car dealerships or real estate, or they can offer general automation services in their local regions.
Another major change will be seen in the business model. As RPA implementation scales, the traditional
per-bot licensing fees can become expensive and limit widespread adoption. Therefore, customers will
prefer subscription-based pricing models. Leading companies such as SAP, Intellibot, and Nintex have
already introduced lower-priced subscription strategies to gain a competitive edge in the increasingly
crowded RPA market.
At the same time, organizations are showing growing interest in the open source approach to manage costs
and encourage innovation. Open source software offers additional advantages, such as building a strong
developer ecosystem that continuously enhances and innovates the platform. Although the current open
source RPA landscape is still fragmented and adoption levels are relatively small, this trend is expected to
evolve rapidly as open source becomes more prominent across industries.
Karjalainen further stated that traditional RPA systems have not encouraged participation from the developer
community because the high cost of entry has limited both market share and profitability. However, as
RPA becomes more open-source driven, developer interest will rise. This will give birth to a new
specialization known as the “RPA developer,” who will focus on creating innovative automation tools and
solutions.
Phil Simpson, Product Marketing Manager for Process Automation at Red Hat, emphasized that open
source is essential for the future of RPA. He said, “Open source is a central pillar of modern cloud stacks,
and if RPA is to have a role in hybrid cloud infrastructure, it must be open source as well.” Although current
open source RPA projects are not yet as comprehensive as proprietary solutions—especially regarding
enterprise-grade support—the industry is steadily moving toward a more open and flexible model. This
progression is expected to bring parity between open source and proprietary RPA systems, ultimately
giving customers greater flexibility and choice in automation technologies.
Chatbots
Chatbots are systems that use Natural Language Processing (NLP) to communicate with people. Although
both chatbots and Robotic Process Automation (RPA) involve software bots, they have distinct functions.
Chatbots are designed to interact and converse with users, while RPA focuses on automating repetitive and
rule-based business processes. In the consumer world, chatbots such as Siri and Cortana are widely used,
while in businesses, they are increasingly adopted for customer service and support functions.
The market for chatbots is growing rapidly. According to Reports and Data, global spending on chatbot
technology is projected to increase from $1.17 billion in 2018 to $10.8 billion by 2026, with a compound
annual growth rate (CAGR) of 30.9%. Major factors driving this growth include the widespread use of
social media platforms and smartphones. Moreover, Juniper Research predicts that chatbot-driven cost
savings will rise from $209 million in 2019 to $7.3 billion by 2023, showing their potential to significantly
impact business efficiency and profitability.
Chatbots can also integrate effectively with RPA systems. Internally, chatbots can act as virtual assistants,
helping employees access information and insights quickly. Externally, they can automate customer
interaction processes, enhancing responsiveness and reducing manual workload.
A practical example of chatbot and RPA integration can be seen in Aflac, a leading provider of financial
protection for health-related issues, serving over 50 million customers. Aflac has been using automation
technologies, including screen scraping, long before RPA became mainstream. In 2017, Aflac implemented
two RPA systems with the help of consultants. The first bot automated wellness claims processing, which
was a quick success, leading to the creation of 28 additional bots. Notably, one employee whose job was
partially automated was later promoted to manage the RPA system.
As Aflac’s automation capabilities matured, the company expanded its focus to chatbot technology.
Initially, it deployed a Facebook Messenger chatbot to answer common customer queries and redirect more
complex issues to human call center agents. However, this system failed because Facebook Messenger could
not identify individual users, making personalized responses impossible. Additionally, the company’s Q&A
database lacked sufficient depth and quality.
Learning from this failure, Aflac revised its strategy. The company introduced a new chatbot system that
allowed employees to answer questions directly, which helped build a more accurate and comprehensive data
foundation. This version included a login system to identify users and a significantly improved database,
increasing the number of question types from 500 to over 20,000.
The result was a major success, with higher customer satisfaction levels and reduced call center traffic
during peak periods. As stated by Keith Farley, Vice President of US Innovation at Aflac, “We realized that
the chatbot was not a replacement for employees. There are times when customers want to talk with a person,
especially in situations involving illness or disease.”
Aflac’s experience demonstrates that while chatbots can greatly improve efficiency and service quality, the
best outcomes are achieved when they are used in combination with human support, ensuring both
automation and empathy in customer interactions.
Data Privacy, Ethics, and AI Regulation
Governments across the world are becoming increasingly proactive in regulating the use of data, particularly
as Artificial Intelligence (AI) continues to evolve into a powerful and influential technology. The European
Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the California Consumer Privacy Act
(CCPA) are two landmark laws that serve as foundational frameworks for future data protection regulations
in other regions and countries.
As Robotic Process Automation (RPA) becomes more advanced and moves beyond simple rules-based
automation, organizations must be especially cautious about compliance with these data privacy laws. Non-
compliance or ethical lapses can result in severe consequences, including hefty fines and reputational
damage.
According to Zachary Jarvinen, Head of Technology Strategy, AI and Analytics at OpenText, the main
objective of data privacy laws is to give consumers the right to know how their personal data is collected
and used, and to provide them the legal authority to take action in cases of bias or data breaches. He further
explains that while most organizations have traditionally focused on structured data, they now need to pay
equal attention to unstructured and textual data that may contain Personally Identifiable Information
(PII).
Jarvinen emphasizes that archived data is a significant concern for many enterprises, as it often holds
sensitive information that is difficult to monitor. To address this, AI-powered solutions can be used to
identify, track, and manage sensitive data through automated workflows. Additionally, organizations must
implement strong data governance frameworks to define accountability for data security and ensure
compliance with privacy regulations. This may include forming specialized teams that combine both
technical and regulatory expertise to oversee enterprise-wide data policies.
Another crucial aspect of ethical AI is the elimination of bias in datasets. Since AI models rely heavily on
data, any inherent bias can lead to unfair or discriminatory outcomes. Therefore, datasets must be cleaned,
diversified, and validated to ensure they represent a wide range of backgrounds and characteristics.
Organizations must also focus on achieving explainability and transparency in their AI systems. Black-box
models that produce results without clarity can lead to mistrust and legal complications. According to
research from DataRobot, 59% of companies plan to invest in more white-box AI systems to improve
explainability, 54% intend to hire internal personnel to manage AI trust, and 48% will rely on third-party
vendors for ensuring ethical AI governance.
Lastly, the use of metadata is emerging as a valuable approach in enhancing data privacy and compliance.
Steve Wood, Chief Product Officer at Boomi, highlights that metadata can support privacy management by
allowing organizations to gain insights into lead generation changes, third-party data access, and
potential breaches. With the help of machine learning and AI modeling, metadata can deliver meaningful
information to business leaders while maintaining compliance with privacy standards and regulations.
